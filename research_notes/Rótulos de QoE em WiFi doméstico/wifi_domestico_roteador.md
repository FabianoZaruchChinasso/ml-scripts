## 1. da Hora, Van Doorselaer, Van Oost, Teixeira: method, label, features, results, limitations, and follow-ups

### Takeaway
The Technicolor + Inria line of work is the closest published analogue to this project. They trained per-application regressors (SVR) in a controlled testbed, mapping passive AP metrics (Tx PHY rate, frame delivery/retransmissions, medium busy split into Wi-Fi vs non-Wi-Fi, RSSI) to an application MOS derived from objective app metrics via standard models (e.g. ITU-T G.1030 for web page load time). They then applied the regressors without ground truth to thousands of ISP APs. The field results (share of "poor QoE" samples) are model outputs with no in-home validation. The web-only predictor had a lab RMSE close to 1 MOS point, which is a large error on a 1-5 scale.

### Cited Findings
**Publications in the line**
- INFOCOM 2018, "Predicting the effect of home Wi-Fi quality on QoE", Honolulu, Apr 2018. Goal: "a system that runs on commodity access points (APs) to assist ISPs in detecting when Wi-Fi degrades QoE". — [HAL hal-01676921 abstract](https://inria.hal.science/hal-01676921)
- Method per the abstract: "passive observation of Wi-Fi quality metrics available in commodity APs (e.g., PHY rate)", "support vector regression to build predictors of QoE given Wi-Fi quality for popular internet applications", then "K-means clustering to combine per-application predictors to identify regions of Wi-Fi quality where QoE is poor across applications. We call samples in these regions as poor QoE samples." — [HAL hal-01676921](https://inria.hal.science/hal-01676921)
- INFOCOM deployment: "Wi-Fi metrics collected over one month from 3479 APs of customers of a large residential ISP". Results: "11.6% of poor QoE samples. Worse, approximately 21% of stations have more than 25% poor QoE samples. ... in most cases poor QoE events are short." — [HAL hal-01676921](https://inria.hal.science/hal-01676921)
- Applications covered: web browsing, YouTube, audio and video real-time communication. — [Thesis abstract, theses.hal.science tel-01670997v2](https://theses.hal.science/tel-01670997v2)
- PhD thesis: Diego Neves da Hora, "Predicting home Wi-Fi QoE from passive measurements on commodity access points", Université Pierre et Marie Curie (Paris VI), 2017, NNT 2017PA066599, Inria MUSE team. Four contributions: (1) a passive Wi-Fi link-capacity estimator that extends 802.11a/b/g models to 802.11n; (2) per-application QoE predictors; (3) K-means poor-QoE event detection, with events classified as "short, intermittent, and consistent"; (4) application to "Wi-Fi metrics collected over one week from 832 APs ... 9% of poor QoE samples ... approximately 10% of stations have more than 25% poor QoE samples." — [theses.hal.science tel-01670997v2](https://theses.hal.science/tel-01670997v2)
- Note the inconsistency: thesis (832 APs, 1 week) reports 9% poor samples and ~10% of stations with >25% poor; INFOCOM (3479 APs, 1 month) reports 11.6% and ~21%. These are different deployments and time spans, and the headline per-station figure doubled. That suggests the prevalence estimate is sensitive to the dataset and threshold, not a stable property. — compare [thesis](https://theses.hal.science/tel-01670997v2) and [INFOCOM abstract](https://inria.hal.science/hal-01676921)
- Precursor: "Predicting the effect of home Wi-Fi quality on Web QoE", ACM SIGCOMM Internet-QoE workshop 2016, Florianópolis, doi 10.1145/2940136.2940146. They instrumented a commercial AP, ran controlled testbed experiments, and built an SVR predictor of web QoE "from metrics that most commercial access points provide". RMSE on MOS: **0.6432 in a controlled environment and 0.9283 in their lab**. Applied to Wi-Fi data from **4,880 APs** in real homes. — [InterDigital abstract page](https://www.interdigital.com/research_papers/predicting-the-effect-of-home-wi-fi-on-web-qoe); [ACM DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2940136.2940146) (full text 403)
- Open-access status: OpenAlex lists green OA copies on HAL for both the INFOCOM paper (hal-01676921) and the 2016 paper (hal-01339522). Citation counts as of query: INFOCOM 2018 = 19, Internet-QoE 2016 = 12 (OpenAlex). Low uptake. — [OpenAlex API](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109/INFOCOM.2018.8486335)

**Method details (from the patent by the same four inventors, priority EP16305390.3, 1 Apr 2016; US11316759B2, assignee listed as Airties Belgium SPRL)**
- Collected AP parameters: actual data rate, PHY data rate, number of spatial streams, channel bandwidth, medium availability, and RSSI. Also "average Tx/Rx PHY rate" and medium unavailability split into Wi-Fi and non-Wi-Fi traffic. — [Google Patents US11316759B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11316759B2/en)
- Feature selection for web browsing kept: **average Tx PHY rate, frame delivery ratio, MediumBusy, MediumBusyOtherWi-Fi**. RSSI was collected but not in the selected web set (per the patent text summary). — [Google Patents US11316759B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11316759B2/en)
- Learning phase: conditions varied along PHY rate (path attenuation of 6, 12, 15, 18, 19, 20 dB) and medium availability (non-Wi-Fi interference emulated by a narrowband sinewave that blocks the AP's CCA; Wi-Fi interference from a second AP/station pair generating traffic). — [Google Patents US11316759B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11316759B2/en)
- Label (web): a station loads pages (e.g. google.com, twitter.com, amazon.fr) 10 times each. Page load time is mapped to a 1-5 MOS "based on ITU-T G.1030", with **MOS 1 = 10 s load time and MOS 5 = the mean page load time** (the anchor is relative to each page's own baseline). An SVR is trained on (link-parameter vector, MOS) pairs. — [Google Patents US11316759B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11316759B2/en)
- Secondary summary lists QoS inputs as "medium busy time, the physical rate of the last frame, frame transmission and retransmission counts, and RSSI". This is consistent with the patent. — search-result summary of [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328378163_Predicting_the_effect_of_home_Wi-Fi_quality_on_QoE) (not opened)

**Limitations visible from what I could read (critical)**
- Ground truth only in the testbed. The field numbers (11.6%, 21%, 9%, 10%) are predictor outputs on unlabeled ISP data, with no in-home QoE validation reported in the abstracts. — [HAL abstract](https://inria.hal.science/hal-01676921)
- The web predictor's lab RMSE of 0.93 MOS (vs 0.64 controlled) shows a large lab-to-less-controlled degradation, even before transfer to real homes. — [InterDigital](https://www.interdigital.com/research_papers/predicting-the-effect-of-home-wi-fi-on-web-qoe)
- The label is an objective-model MOS (G.1030 mapping from PLT), not user ratings. The MOS-5 anchor is the page's mean PLT, so it is relative per page, not per home. — [Patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11316759B2/en)

### Inferences
- The "DMOS" question: I could not confirm that INFOCOM 2018 uses DMOS. Visible evidence points to per-application MOS from standard objective models (G.1030 for web). The PLT-to-MOS anchoring to each page's mean PLT behaves like a "degradation relative to unimpaired baseline" score, which is DMOS-like in spirit, but this is my reading, not their terminology.
- Their feature set maps almost one-to-one to TR-181. Tx PHY ~ `Device.WiFi.AccessPoint.{i}.AssociatedDevice.{i}.LastDataDownlinkRate`; RSSI ~ `...AssociatedDevice.{i}.SignalStrength`; retransmissions/frame delivery ~ `...AssociatedDevice.{i}.Stats.RetransCount`/`PacketsSent` (newer TR-181 versions); medium busy ~ `Device.WiFi.Radio.{i}.Stats` / channel utilization fields (version-dependent). The split of "busy by other Wi-Fi vs non-Wi-Fi" used in their selected features is usually not exposed by TR-181. That is a concrete gap for replicating their best web feature set. The TR-181 parameter names here are from my own knowledge; only SignalStrength (dBm, -200..0, under AssociatedDevice) was confirmed in this session ([CableLabs Wi-Fi provisioning spec summary](https://account.cablelabs.com/server/alfresco/d0690b50-6f52-45a2-8209-ad07f1a7eb10)).
- Their design (train once in a lab, apply everywhere) assumes the mapping from link metrics to QoE does not depend on the home. That is exactly what this project's per-building results contradict (efficiency at equal PHY rate differs by building). The paper therefore gives a feature recipe but no evidence that a lab-trained model transfers across homes.

### Gaps
- Exact INFOCOM 2018 numbers (RMSE per application, YouTube/RTC label models, sampling interval, station/device mix, how K-means thresholds were set) not verified: full text inaccessible in this session (HAL bot wall, IEEE paywall). A human can download hal-01676921v1/file/predicting-effect-home.pdf (2.02 MB) or the thesis PDF 2017PA066599.pdf (3.39 MB) from a browser.
- Whether da Hora or Teixeira later published a follow-up validated against in-home user ratings: none found. Teixeira's related home-network work found is Sundaresan et al. (Section 2).

## 2. Other works: WiSe/Witt, WTF/Home network or access link, WLAN-Probe, WiFi latency (Pei/Sui), Lifemote, operators

### Takeaway
The best-documented AP-side model with real-home ground truth is WiSe/Witt (MobiCom 2013): 30 homes, iperf TCP throughput labels, a product-form "link experience" metric (effective PHY rate x (1 - external airtime) x (1 - local contention)) with r = 0.958, and absolute throughput bands for Very Good to Very Poor. Large-scale latency work (WiFiSeer, campus scale) finds channel utilization, client count and SNR more informative than RSSI. Commercial and ISP work (Lifemote, CPE agents) reports high numbers (R² 0.92) with too little disclosure to judge.

### Cited Findings
**Patro, Govindan, Banerjee, "Observing home wireless experience through WiFi APs", ACM MobiCom 2013, pp. 339-350, doi 10.1145/2500423.2500448 (WiSe)**
- 30 OpenWrt APs given to residents as their primary AP. Traces from "30 homes for a period in excess of 6 months". Two-thirds of APs were concentrated in two apartment buildings. More than 100 GB of data. — [author PDF](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- AP-side stats: airtime utilization (incl. external Wi-Fi and non-Wi-Fi), CRC errors, per-client packets sent/received/retried, per-packet PHY rate, retry bit and RSSI, non-Wi-Fi device detection (Airshark). Collected in 10 s intervals for the ground-truth correlation. — [author PDF](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- Label/ground truth: 4 laptops at 8 deployment locations in the apartment buildings ran 20-s iperf TCP downlink tests (AP-to-client, so the Wi-Fi link is the bottleneck) every 5-10 minutes for a week, "hundreds of these measurements", at different times of day and connected to different APs. — [author PDF](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- Correlation with measured TCP throughput (802.11g): airtime 0.321, CRC errors 0.345, local contention 0.463, **signal strength 0.536**, **effective rate 0.882**, effective rate + airtime 0.915, link_exp model **0.958**. — [author PDF, Table 4](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- Model: effective rate r = sum(s_i * r_i) / sum(p_i) over PHY rates (success-weighted PHY rate). link_exp = (1 - a)(1 - c) r, where a = external airtime and c = local contention (share of AP traffic for other clients). Witt = β1·link_exp + β0, with separate fits per standard: 802.11g (0.167, 0.422), 802.11n (-0.493, 0.733). Train and test sets split by day. Error under 1.5 Mbps (g) and 2 Mbps (n) for more than 80% of instances. — [author PDF, Eq. 1-3, Table 5, Fig. 5](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- Categorical rating (absolute thresholds): Witt >= 16 Mbps Very Good, 8-16 Good, 4-8 Moderate, 1-4 Poor, < 1 Very Poor. Bands double at each step. Max Witt observed ~19 Mbps (g), 34 Mbps (n). — [author PDF, Table 7](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- Results: over 80 days, poor client experience about 2.1% of active periods. Very Poor periods were rare; Poor periods were intermittent "depending on the link and the location". — [author PDF](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- Per-building heterogeneity: the dominant cause of Very Poor periods differed by building. In Bldg 1, high airtime (>60%) + high MAC loss (>50%) caused 61.8%. In Bldg 2, low signal (<-70 dBm) + loss + low PHY (<=12 Mbps) caused 51.6%. — [author PDF, Table 8](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- Ground-truth parameter ranges: airtime 17.6-72.5%, CRC errors 1.2-98.8%, MAC retries 6.2-86.4%, signal -71 to -49 dBm (median -61). The ground truth never covered signals below -71 dBm. — [author PDF, Table 6](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- Other: interference periods mostly short (1-7 min); microwave ovens degraded Witt by up to 81% at some APs; 56% of observed home APs kept static channels over 30 days; 802.11n did not guarantee higher throughput than 802.11g for the same link. — [author PDF](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- Data release page exists. — [WiSe data release](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~patro/wise_datarelease/index.html)

**Sundaresan, Feamster, Teixeira, "Home Network or Access Link? Locating Last-Mile Downstream Throughput Bottlenecks", PAM 2016, LNCS 9631, pp. 111-123, doi 10.1007/978-3-319-30505-9_9 (full text not read)**
- Passive, lightweight metrics on the home gateway (BISmark) to say whether the bottleneck is inside the home (wireless) or the access link. Validated in controlled settings. Two deployments, one with 2,652 homes across the US. Finding: wireless bottlenecks are more common than access-link bottlenecks, especially for homes with downstream throughput above 20 Mbps. — [Princeton listing summary](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/home-network-or-access-link-locating-last-mile-downstream-through/) (via search summary)
- Detector (from slides, unverified): the coefficient of variation of packet inter-arrival time at the gateway (cvt) is low when the access link is the bottleneck (packets are smoothed). The LAN RTT between gateway and client rises at a wireless bottleneck. Reported thresholds were Tc = 0.8 (cvt) and Tt = 15 ms (RTT), with a demand check (> 10 packets/s) before classifying. Slide versions differ in decision order. — [NANOG64 slides](https://www.nanog.org/sites/default/files/meetings/NANOG64/1012/20150601_Sundaresan_Home_Network_Or_v1.pdf) (402 on fetch; via search summary); [CAIDA AIMS 2014 slides](https://www-old.caida.org/workshops/aims/1403/slides/aims1403_nfeamster.pdf)

**Kanuparthy, Dovrolis, Papagiannaki, Seshan, Steenkiste, "Can User-Level Probing Detect and Diagnose Common Home-WLAN Pathologies?", ACM CCR 42(1):7-15, 2012 (arXiv:1108.2070, 2011)**
- Client-side, user-level UDP packet trains (100 trains x 50 packets) to an Ethernet-attached server. Estimates "wireless access delay" and infers the 802.11 rate from packet-pair dispersion. A decision tree separates low SNR, symmetric hidden terminals, and congestion. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1108.2070)
- Evaluation: an 802.11g testbed only (Soekris nodes, Atheros/Intel, channel 6, MadWiFi), e.g. 100 low-SNR and 90 hidden-terminal experiments. No home deployment. About 10-20% of low-SNR cases were misdiagnosed as hidden terminals. No end-to-end detection or false-positive rate. It "cannot yet distinguish normal operation from pathology". — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1108.2070)
- It deliberately avoids NIC/driver statistics to avoid "calibrat[ing] NIC statistics across devices and drivers". Cross-home robustness was not evaluated. — [arXiv 1108.2070](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.2070)

**Pei et al., "WiFi Can Be the Weakest Link of Round Trip Network Latency in the Wild", IEEE INFOCOM 2016, doi 10.1109/INFOCOM.2016.7524396 (full text not read)**
- 47 Netgear WNDR4300 OpenWrt APs on Tsinghua campus (44 in dormitories, 3 in the CS department), May 20 to July 20, ~2 TB of traces. WiLy measures the Wi-Fi hop latency on the AP from TCP handshakes. More than 50% (10%) of TCP packets had Wi-Fi hop latency > 20 ms (100 ms). For about half of packets, the Wi-Fi hop was > 60% of the round-trip network latency. — [slides transcription (sambuz)](https://api.sambuz.com/doc/wifi-can-be-the-weakest-link-of-round-trip-network-ppt-presentation-799619); [JIO searchworks record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/edb__118372672)
- Model (from slides, unverified): a decision tree labels samples FAST/SLOW with accuracy 0.78 downlink, 0.68 uplink, 0.77 combined; a random forest > 0.8. The strongest factor was airtime utilization (Kendall ~0.86). There was no strong latency effect when AU < 0.5, Tx PHY > 60 Mbps or RSSI > -60 dBm. — [slides transcription](https://api.sambuz.com/doc/wifi-can-be-the-weakest-link-of-round-trip-network-ppt-presentation-799619)

**Sui, Zhou, Liu, Ma, Pei, Zhao, Li, Moscibroda, "Characterizing and Improving WiFi Latency in Large-Scale Operational Networks", ACM MobiSys 2016 (WiFiSeer; full text read)**
- Campus enterprise WLAN (not homes): more than 2,700 APs, 114 buildings, more than 47,000 devices. Device-side latency is measured by "ping2", joined with controller SNMP data (radio factors such as channel utilization, protocol factors such as band/channel, environmental factors such as building, AP model, device vendor via OUI). — [MSR PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/mobisys_2016b.pdf)
- Latency p50/p90/p99 ≈ 3/20/250 ms. 75% of devices had at least one "unsatisfying episode" (> 20 ms average for > 1 min) per week. 5 GHz gives lower latency but is underused, and device band selection is biased to 2.4 GHz. — [MSR PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/mobisys_2016b.pdf)
- "The three most informative indicators for WiFi latency are channel utilization, the number of online devices, and the signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR), while the commonly considered RSSI used for AP selection is not." Relationships are "non-linear or even non-monotonic". — [MSR PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/mobisys_2016b.pdf)
- Uses classification instead of regression for the operational apps (decision trees for interpretability, random forests where precision/recall matter). The AP-selection service (> 1,000 devices, 2.5 months) cut latency by over half for 72% of re-associations. — [MSR PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/mobisys_2016b.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a speed-test-labelled project, WiSe is the most directly comparable published design. Its ground truth is AP-to-client TCP throughput, it uses absolute throughput bands, and its features are AP-side. Its key finding is that a success-weighted PHY rate times (1 - busy) times (1 - contention) beats any single metric, with RSSI alone only r = 0.54. This matches the project's observation that PHY rate dominates and RSSI is weaker.
- WiSe needed **separate linear coefficients for 802.11g and 802.11n** (slope 0.42 vs 0.73). That is effectively a per-technology efficiency factor, the same phenomenon as the project's per-building "efficiency at the same PHY rate". By analogy, it suggests calibrating efficiency per AP model/standard/band (e.g. AX vs non-AX) rather than hoping one global mapping fits.
- WiSe ground truth covered signals only down to -71 dBm. Their thresholds and fit say nothing about the deep-coverage-hole regime, which is where "bad rooms" live.
- Accuracy numbers across works are not comparable: RMSE on MOS (da Hora), correlation with TCP throughput (WiSe), FAST/SLOW accuracy (Pei), R² (Lifemote). None reports per-home or leave-one-home-out generalization in the material I read.

### Gaps
- No public, verifiable large-scale study from Comcast, Charter, Google (Nest Wifi), Meta or Plume linking router telemetry to measured QoE was found in this session. NCTA/SCTE technical papers (e.g. "Enhancing Wi-Fi QoE with targeted approach", 2018) exist, but the fetch returned only a landing page.
- Plume: nothing citable found (not searched in depth due to the tool budget).
- Sundaresan PAM 2016 and Pei INFOCOM 2016 full texts not read. Thresholds and accuracies above come from slides.

## 3. Handling heterogeneity between homes; relative vs absolute labels

### Takeaway
None of the works I could read normalizes features per home or validates leave-one-home-out. Heterogeneity is handled by (a) lab-trained models applied as-is (da Hora), (b) separate fits per 802.11 standard plus absolute throughput bands (WiSe), (c) self-normalizing signals that need no absolute calibration (Sundaresan's cvt and LAN-RTT; Kanuparthy's user-level timing), or (d) environment and device identity as explicit features (WiFiSeer: building, AP model, device vendor). Labels are mostly absolute (Mbps bands, MOS via absolute models, 20 ms latency). The one relative element is da Hora's web MOS anchored to each page's mean PLT.

### Cited Findings
- da Hora: SVR trained in a testbed and applied to 3,479 (or 832, or 4,880) ISP APs. No per-home calibration is mentioned in the abstracts or the patent ("Not specified: per-home or per-device calibration"). — [HAL abstract](https://inria.hal.science/hal-01676921); [Patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11316759B2/en)
- da Hora web label: MOS 5 anchored to a page's mean PLT and MOS 1 to 10 s (G.1030). Relative to the page, absolute across homes. — [Patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11316759B2/en)
- WiSe: separate linear coefficients for 802.11g vs 802.11n. Absolute Witt bands (>=16, 8-16, 4-8, 1-4, <1 Mbps). Per-AP Witt averaged over active clients. Train/test split by day, not by home. — [WiSe PDF](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- WiSe: dominant causes of poor periods differed by building (airtime+loss 61.8% in Bldg 1 vs low signal+loss+low PHY 51.6% in Bldg 2). — [WiSe PDF, Table 8](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- Sundaresan et al.: they condition on access-link tier and report that wireless bottlenecks dominate above 20 Mbps downstream. A Wi-Fi link is "bad" only relative to the home's access link (relative-to-home definition of the problem). — [Princeton listing (summary)](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/home-network-or-access-link-locating-last-mile-downstream-through/)
- Kanuparthy et al.: avoid NIC stats explicitly to avoid cross-device calibration. Heterogeneity across homes was not evaluated. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1108.2070)
- WiFiSeer: includes building, building type, AP model and device vendor as features. Finds non-monotonic metric-latency relationships and that RSSI is weakly informative. — [MSR PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/mobisys_2016b.pdf)
- Lifemote claims models learn "from individual CPE performance and across the entire CPE population", i.e. per-CPE baselines plus population models. No published method or evaluation found. — [Lifemote blog](https://www.lifemote.com/post/https-lifemote-com-2023-08-17-how-can-lifemote-improve-your-isp-success-metrics)
- RSSI is a weak predictor of PHY rate/throughput across locations (60 GHz lab study, different band). This is background support for not relying on RSSI thresholds across homes. — [arXiv 1509.04274](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.04274)

### Inferences
- The literature offers no tested recipe for per-home normalization of AP features. Sundaresan's relative-to-access-link framing is the clearest precedent for a "relative" label. For this project, that supports a label like "room throughput / building or plan reference" alongside an absolute floor. That is close to the project's hybrid ruler, but no paper validates such a hybrid.
- WiFiSeer's "environment as a feature" approach only works when there are many environments (114 buildings). With ~6 buildings, a building ID feature would overfit or be useless for unseen buildings. The defensible analogues are a per-AP-model/standard efficiency factor (WiSe-style) or a per-building reference measured at install time.
- Because published accuracies come from splits by time/day or from lab data, the project's leave-one-building-out results are not directly comparable and are likely harsher. That point is worth stating when comparing to reported 0.92 R² or 0.96 correlation.

### Gaps
- No paper found reports leave-one-home-out or per-home-normalized results for router-side Wi-Fi QoE prediction.
- da Hora's full INFOCOM text might discuss per-device effects (e.g. station capability); not verified.

## 4. Disconnections/failures missing from logs, and how they were captured

### Takeaway
None of the academic works I read measures disconnections or failed sessions as a QoE outcome. Their pipelines condition on "active" associated clients with traffic (WiSe analyzes only periods with at least one active client; da Hora's samples come from transmitting stations; Pei/WiFiSeer measure latency of connected devices). Periods when a client cannot connect, or connects without passing traffic, are invisible by construction. The only explicit treatment found is in patents and vendor documentation.

### Cited Findings
- WiSe analysis restricted to periods when the AP has at least one active client; poor experience (2.1%) is "of the active periods". — [WiSe PDF](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- WiSe uses client RSSI as seen at the AP as a proxy for client-side link quality "since we do not have access to the clients". — [WiSe PDF](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~suman/pubs/wise.pdf)
- WLAN-Probe aborts diagnosis when rate inference fails (most trains lack a strong rate mode). Failures produce no diagnosis rather than a "bad" label. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1108.2070)
- WiFiSeer: ping2 latency is measured on devices that are associated. The paper discusses device energy-saving modes as a measurement confounder. — [MSR PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/mobisys_2016b.pdf)
- A Wi-Fi QoE patent counts temporary client/AP disconnections as an input that lowers a device's QoE score: "QoE ... can be seriously degraded if the client device or AP ... disconnects temporarily". — [USPTO 11159965](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11159965) (via search summary)
- AP-side logs mislabel client-side failures. A client that stops communicating without notifying the AP is logged with "unknown reason". Clients in sleep mode that turn off the radio look like normal departures. — [Meraki wireless event log docs](https://documentation.meraki.com/Wireless/Troubleshooting_and_Support/Troubleshooting/Common_Wireless_Event_Log_Messages_and_Issues)
- Root-causing association rejects needs both device logs and AP logs. — [Samsung Knox docs](https://docs.samsungknox.com/admin/knox-asset-intelligence/dashboard/network-insights/wifi-disconnection-events/)

### Inferences
- Router-side TR-181 snapshots have the same blind spot. AssociatedDevice entries exist only for associated stations, so a room where the phone drops off or never associates produces no row, not a "bad" row. Unless the collector records test failures/timeouts on the client side and keeps them as labelled "bad" samples, the dataset is biased toward the better rooms. This is consistent with the project memory note that the deadzone signal must come from the collector.
- Published prevalence figures (2.1% WiSe, 9-11.6% da Hora) are therefore lower bounds on poor experience. They exclude disconnection and non-association periods.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found that quantifies how much poor home Wi-Fi experience appears as disconnection/non-association missing from AP telemetry, or that compares AP-side telemetry against client-side failure logs at home scale.
