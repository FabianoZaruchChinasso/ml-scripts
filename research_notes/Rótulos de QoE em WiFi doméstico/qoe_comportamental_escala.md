## 1. Video: how buffering ratio, startup delay and bitrate affect engagement, and whether the relationship is linear, thresholded or saturating

### Takeaway
Across three large Conviva and Akamai datasets plus one randomized trial, rebuffering is the quality metric with the most consistent negative effect on engagement. Startup delay has a clear threshold at about 2 s, beyond which abandonment rises roughly linearly. Bitrate has a non-monotonic relationship with engagement because it is confounded with bitrate switching. Rebuffering hurts roughly linearly only at low buffering ratios (0–10%), and the buffering-rate effect has a knee at about 0.3 events/min. Per-session effects are small (a few percent of play time). They became visible only with millions of sessions, and the one RCT showed that even years of data leave wide confidence intervals.

### Cited Findings
**Dobrian et al., SIGCOMM 2011 (Conviva, full text)**
- Dataset: client-side instrumentation in the video player measuring join time, buffering ratio (BufRatio), average bitrate, rendering quality (RendQual) and rate of buffering events (RateBuf). The paper reports "summary results from over 2 million unique views from over 1 million viewers" across live, long VoD and short VoD from several mainstream providers. — [Dobrian 2011 PDF](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p362.pdf)
- Headline effect: "BufRatio consistently has the highest impact on user engagement among all quality metrics. For example, for a 90 minutes live event, an increase of BufRatio by 1% can decrease PlayTime by over 3 minutes." — [Dobrian 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p362.pdf)
- Linearity holds only in a restricted range. The authors "only apply regression to the 0-10% range of BufRatio, where we confirmed a simple linear relationship". Median linear-fit slope magnitudes were about 1 for long VoD, 2 for live, and close to zero for short VoD. They explicitly avoid "blindly applying quantitative regression models" because the relationships "can be quite complex". — [Dobrian 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p362.pdf)
- Non-monotone metrics: for long VoD, average bitrate had a weak Kendall correlation but high information gain "because the relationship was non-monotone". The authors recommend using several complementary analysis techniques, for example correlation plus information gain. — [Dobrian 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p362.pdf)
- Base rates in one dataset (LvodA): "7% of views experience BufRatio larger than 10%, 5% of views have JoinTime larger than 10s, and 37% of views have RendQual lower than 90%". Most sessions are good, so the bad tail is a small minority. — [Dobrian 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p362.pdf)
- Level of aggregation changes conclusions. At the viewer level, "reducing BufRatio by 1% translates to an effective increase the total play time by 1.2 minutes for long VoD content and by 2.4 minutes for live content on average per user". Join time, "which seemed less relevant at the view level, has non-trivial impact at the viewer level", meaning it affects whether users return. — [Dobrian 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p362.pdf)
- Method caveat: Kendall correlations are computed on **binned means** (average play time per 1% BufRatio bin), not on individual sessions. The very high values shown in figures (e.g., −0.94 and −0.97) are therefore bin-level and say nothing about per-session predictability. — [Dobrian 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p362.pdf)

**Krishnan & Sitaraman, IMC 2012 (Akamai, full text)**
- Dataset: "more than 23 million video playbacks from 6.7 million unique viewers who watched an aggregate of 216 million minutes of 102 thousand videos over 10 days". — [Krishnan & Sitaraman 2012 PDF](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~ramesh/Site/HOME_files/imc208-krishnan.pdf)
- Startup delay threshold: the abandonment rate "is near zero for the first 2 seconds, but starts to rise rapidly as the startup delay increases. Fitting a simple regression to initial part of the curve shows that abandonment rate increases by 5.8% for each 1-second increase in startup delay." The paper does not say whether "5.8%" means percentage points or relative change. — [Krishnan & Sitaraman 2012](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~ramesh/Site/HOME_files/imc208-krishnan.pdf)
- Rebuffering (causal design): "a viewer who experienced a rebuffer delay that equals or exceeds 1% of the video duration played 5.02% less of the video in comparison to a similar viewer who experienced no rebuffering". The net outcome shows "a general upward trend … when the treatment gets harsher". The plain correlational Kendall between normalized rebuffer delay and play time was −0.421. — [Krishnan & Sitaraman 2012](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~ramesh/Site/HOME_files/imc208-krishnan.pdf)
- Return visits (churn proxy): the probability of returning within 1 day was 8.0% after a failed visit vs 11% after a normal one, and within 1 week 25% vs 27%. In the QED, failure lowers the likelihood of returning within a week by 2.32%. — [Krishnan & Sitaraman 2012](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~ramesh/Site/HOME_files/imc208-krishnan.pdf)
- Context moderates tolerance. Viewers of short videos are less patient than viewers of long videos (net outcome 11.5%). "Viewers on mobile devices have the most patience and abandon the least, while those on fiber-based broadband abandon the soonest" (net outcome 38.25%). — [Krishnan & Sitaraman 2012](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~ramesh/Site/HOME_files/imc208-krishnan.pdf)
- Note on the metric: QED "net outcome" is the share of matched pairs that support the hypothesis minus the share that contradict it. It is not an effect size on abandonment, so 38.25% does not mean fiber users abandon 38% more. — [Krishnan & Sitaraman 2012](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~ramesh/Site/HOME_files/imc208-krishnan.pdf)

**Balachandran et al., SIGCOMM 2013 (Conviva, full text)**
- Dataset: "40 million video viewing sessions collected over 3 months spanning two popular video content providers (based in the US)". — [Balachandran 2013 PDF](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2013/papers/sigcomm/p339.pdf)
- Explicit threshold and non-monotonicity: "engagement linearly decreases with increasing rate of buffering up to a certain threshold (0.3 buffering events/minute). Beyond this, users get annoyed and they quit early". The bitrate–engagement relationship is "non-monotonic" because intermediate average bitrates mark sessions that switched bitrates and probably buffered more. — [Balachandran 2013](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2013/papers/sigcomm/p339.pdf)
- Predictability: engagement was cast as classes of the fraction of video watched (e.g., 10 classes = 10% bins). A decision tree beat naive Bayes and linear regression ("linear regression is not expressible enough to capture the complex relationships"), reaching "close to 45% accuracy". Splitting by confounders raised this to "as much as 70%", meaning "about 70% of the predictions are within the same 10% bucket as the actual user viewing duration". — [Balachandran 2013](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2013/papers/sigcomm/p339.pdf)
- Main confounders: "Type of video (live vs. VOD), device (PC vs. mobile devices vs. TV) and connectivity (cable/DSL vs. wireless) are the three most important confounding factors". They can affect engagement, the quality metric itself, or the quality→engagement relationship. For example, "users on wireless connectivity are more tolerant to rate of buffering compared to users on DSL/cable". — [Balachandran 2013](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2013/papers/sigcomm/p339.pdf)
- Limitations the authors state: fraction viewed "does not capture various psychological factors" (e.g., video playing in the background). Unmeasured confounders exist (e.g., user interest). "Early quitters" may be sampling content or quitting because of quality, and the authors **removed them** from the analysis. — [Balachandran 2013](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2013/papers/sigcomm/p339.pdf)

**Yan et al., "Learning in situ: a randomized experiment in video streaming" (Puffer), NSDI 2020 (full text)**
- Design: a blinded RCT that randomizes sessions across ABR algorithms. "38.6 years of video to 63,508 registered study participants using 111,231 unique IP addresses". The primary analysis covers 637,189 eligible streams. — [Puffer NSDI 2020 PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/nsdi20-paper-yan.pdf)
- Stalls are rare: "only 24,328 (4%) of those streams had any stalls, mirroring commercial services". — [Puffer 2020](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/nsdi20-paper-yan.pdf)
- Statistical power is the main lesson: "with at least 2.5 years of data for each scheme, the width of the 95% confidence interval on a scheme's stall ratio is between ±13% and ±21% of the mean value", and "even with a year of experience per scheme, a 20% improvement in rebuffering ratio would be statistically indistinguishable". A Facebook study with 30 million streams "did not detect a change in rebuffering ratio outside the level of statistical noise". — [Puffer 2020](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/nsdi20-paper-yan.pdf)
- Engagement outcome: users randomly assigned to Fugu "chose to continue streaming for 5–9% longer". The footnote adds that "This effect was driven solely by users streaming more than 3 hours of video; we do not fully understand it." Engagement is therefore heavy-tailed and dominated by a few heavy users. — [Puffer 2020](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/nsdi20-paper-yan.pdf)

**Plakia et al., arXiv 1901.01603, 2019 (abstract only)**
- On two large YouTube datasets, controlling for video duration and mean data rate, "the number of negative bitrate changes (BR-) is a stronger predictor of abandonment than rebufferrings". Positive switches also correlate with abandonment in low-resolution sessions, and the timing of impairments matters. Sample sizes are not in the abstract. — [arXiv 1901.01603](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.01603)

### Inferences
- The shape is **thresholded at low impairment, then roughly linear, and possibly saturating at the extreme**: near-zero abandonment for the first ~2 s of startup, a linear decline in engagement up to ~0.3 buffering events/min with a steeper quit beyond it, and linearity of BufRatio only over 0–10%. Nobody fits a global linear model, and Balachandran shows linear regression underperforms trees. This supports the project's current move to a direct classifier over a linear ruler.
- Bitrate, the closest analogue to Wi-Fi PHY rate or throughput, is non-monotonic in these data because it is confounded with switching. The same trap applies to throughput-like features from the router.
- Effects per session are small and noisy. The strongest engagement effects quoted (5% less play time, 2.3% lower return rate, 3 minutes out of 90) were detectable only with 10^7 sessions or matched designs over millions of viewers.

### Gaps
- Netflix and YouTube internal follow-ups (e.g., Netflix tech-blog engagement studies) were not opened. I found no peer-reviewed Netflix paper with engagement-vs-network effect sizes in this pass.
- Conviva follow-ups after 2013 (e.g., Jiang et al. CFA, NSDI 2016) were not opened. They use the same engagement labels and add no new behavioural-label methodology to what is above.
- Plakia et al.'s full text (sample sizes, effect sizes) was not read.

## 2. Video and audio calls: in-product ratings vs network metrics, and how much of the rating network explains

### Takeaway
Skype and Microsoft Teams-family data show that the share of poor ratings (PCR, the share of 1–2 star calls) rises steadily with RTT, loss and jitter, with no plateau. The very high correlations (~0.9+) are computed on binned call populations. Per call, telemetry predicts a poor rating only moderately: the best figure I found is AUC 0.74 from 40 telemetry features in a Microsoft CMC system, and the authors blame per-call aggregation for part of the gap. Ratings are sparse (shown on a small random fraction of calls), skewed toward 5 stars, and sensitive to survey design.

### Cited Findings
**Jiang et al., "VIA: Improving Internet Telephony Call Quality Using Predictive Relay Selection", SIGCOMM 2016 (Skype, full text)**
- Dataset: "a sampled set of 430 million audio calls drawn from a seven month period", from "1,905 ASes across 126 countries". 46.6% of calls are international, 80.7% inter-AS and **83% wireless**. — [VIA PDF (author copy)](https://cs.cmu.edu/~junchenj/via.pdf); [MSR page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/via-improving-internet-telephony-call-quality-using-predictive-relay-selection/)
- Rating mechanism: "For a small random fraction of calls in Skype, users label the call quality on a discrete 5-point scale… we deem the calls with a rating of 1 or 2 as 'poor', and use the fraction of such calls, termed as the Poor Call Rate (PCR)". The response rate and the absolute PCR are not given, and the plots are normalized to the maximum PCR. — [VIA](https://cs.cmu.edu/~junchenj/via.pdf)
- Shape: binning calls with at least 1,000 samples per bin, "PCR significantly increases with all the three network metrics (correlation coefficients of 0.97, 0.95, 0.91)… PCR is sensitive to the entire spectrum of network metrics". This describes a smooth, monotone dose–response on population PCR, not a step. — [VIA](https://cs.cmu.edu/~junchenj/via.pdf)
- Operational thresholds: RTT > 320 ms, loss > 1.2%, jitter > 12 ms count as "poor network". Over 15% of calls exceed at least one of them. All are call-averaged values, so "transient spikes (e.g., loss burst)" are hidden. A check on 70k calls with full packet traces found that 80% of calls rated "non-poor" by the thresholds had a higher packet-trace MOS than 75% of calls rated "poor". — [VIA](https://cs.cmu.edu/~junchenj/via.pdf)
- Wireless last hop: the authors focus on the WAN path but note that "the wireless last hop could be a significant contributor to poor call quality", and that with a poor last hop "no relaying strategy can help". — [VIA](https://cs.cmu.edu/~junchenj/via.pdf)

**Gupchup et al., "Analysis of Problem Tokens to Rank Factors Impacting Quality in VoIP Applications", QoMEX 2017 (Skype, full text via ar5iv)**
- About 700,000 unique one-to-one calls from more than 100,000 users over two weeks. After a rating below 5 an optional "problem token questionnaire" is shown, and about 54% of users who rated a call poor selected at least one token. — [arXiv 1805.00373](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.00373); [MSR page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=812551)
- Five problem groups (audio quality 26%, video quality 25%, one-way video 12%, one-way audio 11%, reliability 7%) explain 81% of token variability. Reliability tokens ("no sound", "unexpected call end", "no video") had the largest impact on **average call duration**. Distorted speech and frozen video affect PCR more than call duration. A model on tokens reached 93% TPR at the baseline 10.8% FPR (AUC 0.95) for poor-call status. — [arXiv 1805.00373](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.00373)
- The paper does **not** relate tokens or ratings to network telemetry, and it reports strong response bias (users answer far more after poor calls). — [arXiv 1805.00373](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.00373)

**Hosseinkashi et al., "Meeting Effectiveness and Inclusiveness…", CSCW 2024 (Microsoft CMC system, full text)**
- Call-quality classifier: "Quality Issues is a binary classifier that consumes 40 telemetry statistics about issues such as echo, noise, or speech distortions… trained to predict the probability of poor CQF rating (rating 1 or 2…)… This classifier has 74% AUC on the validation set. A 74% AUC is considered good performance when using call telemetry to predict real user ratings… Call telemetry is aggregated statistics and does not fully capture the changes in quality during the call." — [arXiv 2304.00652](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.00652)
- Reliability vs quality: "Reliability Issues" (call drop, one-way audio) is a simple binary flag from client telemetry with no ML needed. Quality issues are associated with OR = 0.1 for an "Effective" meeting rating. — [arXiv 2304.00652](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.00652)
- Rating skew and survey design (randomized experiments, ~8k and 4.6k users): a one-page survey yields "more distributed ratings" (fewer 5-star "Perfect Meeting Rate"). A high trigger rate without cool-down "can lead to more skewed ratings". The final design used a 10% trigger rate and a 7-day cool-down. The final dataset has 15K ratings. Models predicting a **specific attendee's** rating did worse than non-attendee-specific ones, and transfer across organizations lost "considerable" accuracy. — [arXiv 2304.00652](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.00652)

**Chen et al., "Quantifying Skype User Satisfaction", SIGCOMM 2006 (search summary only; PDF blocked)**
- Proposes a User Satisfaction Index built from **call duration** in passive Skype traces as an implicit satisfaction label, using bit rate, bit-rate jitter and RTT, with no surveys. I could not open the paper, so no numbers are reported here. — [Author page (HTTP 500 when fetched)](https://homepage.iis.sinica.edu.tw/~swc/pub/skype_user_satisfaction.html); DOI 10.1145/1159913.1159959

**Industry, secondary (weak evidence)**
- A No Jitter column by Kieran Kelly claims that, on data from more than 100,000 Skype for Business users, network MOS only weakly influences perceived call quality, and that two users on the same call can rate it 5 and 3 stars. This is trade press, not peer-reviewed. — [No Jitter](https://www.nojitter.com/video-conferencing/sophisticated-skype-good-mos-does-not-make-a-good-call)
- Microsoft's Message Center item MC1183608 (2025–2026) describes moving Teams "Rate my call" to a thumbs up/down survey with problem tokens, and warns that reported poor calls may rise because of reporting changes, not service changes. I only saw a search summary. — [MC1183608](https://mc.merill.net/message/MC1183608)

### Inferences
- "How much of the rating does network explain" has no clean R² in these papers. The best per-call figure is AUC ≈ 0.74 for a binary poor/not-poor rating from rich telemetry. Even at hyperscale, roughly a quarter of the ranking signal is missing, because of per-user bias, content and context, and the loss of within-call dynamics when telemetry is averaged.
- Binning many calls and correlating bin means (VIA: r ≈ 0.95) hides per-call noise. A small project with one rating per room cannot get those correlations: the noise that averages out over thousands of calls stays in each individual label.
- Reliability events (drop, one-way audio, no video) are detectable without ML and move behaviour (call duration) the most. They are the cheapest and cleanest implicit labels.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed large-n Zoom or Google Meet study linking in-product ratings or early leave to network metrics. Searches returned lab studies (MacMillan et al., IMC 2021) and campus passive measurement (Zoom PAM 2022), neither of which has user behaviour as the outcome.
- No paper gave the response rate to Skype or Teams rating prompts.

## 3. Web and mobile apps: abandonment and usage vs latency and radio quality

### Takeaway
The cleanest real-user evidence is Google's randomized delay injection. Adding 100–400 ms of server delay cut daily searches per user by 0.2–0.6%. The effect grew with exposure and partly persisted after the delay was removed. Cellular-operator studies (Balachandran MobiCom 2014, Shafiq SIGMETRICS 2014) infer abandonment from network traces and find that radio factors (SINR, handovers, load) matter more than nominal link rate. Their full texts were inaccessible.

### Cited Findings
- **Brutlag, "Speed Matters for Google Web Search", 2009 (full text).** Randomized experiment and control groups, 4–6 weeks. Pre-header 100 ms: −0.20% daily searches per user. Post-header 200 ms: −0.29%. Post-header 400 ms: −0.59%. Post-ads 200 ms: −0.30%. A 50 ms delay had no measurable effect ("—"). Over 6 weeks, the second half showed −0.36% and −0.74% (200 and 400 ms) vs −0.22% and −0.44% in the first half. After the 400 ms delay was removed, searches stayed −0.21% vs control over the next 5 weeks. — [Google PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/blogs/google_delayexp.pdf)
- **Balachandran et al., "Modeling Web Quality-of-Experience on Cellular Networks", MobiCom 2014 (abstract only).** Uses a month of anonymized data from a major cellular provider. Because operators lack client and server logs, QoE metrics (abandonment, session length) are first **inferred from network traces with ML**. Findings: better SNR, lower load and fewer handovers improve QoE; QoE is "highly sensitive" to inter-RAT handovers; and "a higher radio data link rate does not necessarily mean better QoE". — [CMU page](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~srini/papers/video/2014-Balachandran-mobicom/); DOI 10.1145/2639108.2639137
- **Shafiq et al., "Understanding the Impact of Network Dynamics on Mobile Video User Engagement", SIGMETRICS 2014 (snippets and patent summary only).** Month-long data set from a major cellular network. It quantifies "the effect that 31 different cellular network factors have on video abandonment rate and video skip (e.g., fast forward) rate", and argues an operator can monitor engagement from radio statistics and/or TCP/IP flow records alone. The companion AT&T patent states that "improving mean signal-to-interference ratio (EcIo) by 1 dB reduces the likelihood of video abandonment by 2%". I could not verify this against the paper text. — [SciSpace link (empty when fetched)](https://typeset.io/pdf/understanding-the-impact-of-network-dynamics-on-mobile-video-5d3qix6z9h.pdf); [Patent 10505833](https://patents.justia.com/patent/10505833)

### Inferences
- The Google numbers show how small behavioural effects of latency are: fractions of a percent per user per day. Detecting them required Google-scale randomized traffic. Behaviour of this kind is unusable as a per-session label.
- "Link rate ≠ QoE" (MobiCom 2014) parallels this project's finding that PHY rate alone does not set QoE and efficiency differs per building.

### Gaps
- The full texts of MobiCom 2014 and SIGMETRICS 2014 (ACM DL 403; SciSpace returned empty bodies). Their quantitative effects, model accuracies and handling of confounders are unverified.
- Akamai and industry web-abandonment statistics (e.g., "100 ms costs X% conversions") are marketing material and were deliberately not used. "The impact of network latency on user engagement" as an exact title was not located.

## 4. Gaming: departure and session length vs latency, jitter and loss

### Takeaway
The only large real-user gaming study I could read (ShenZhou Online MMORPG, Chen et al.) finds that loss and jitter drive premature departure much more than mean latency. The 2009 version reports that network quality explains 32% of the variability in leaving within 10 minutes. The effect fades the longer a player stays. The study is cross-sectional, and the relative-weight ratios changed between paper versions.

### Cited Findings
- **Chen, Huang, Wang, Huang, Lei, INFOCOM 2006 (full text).** Survival analysis (Cox proportional hazards) on "a 1,356-million-packet trace" from ShenZhou Online: "15,140 sessions were observed with 5,105 sessions censored", median session time 100 min. "The degrees of player 'intolerance' of minimum RTT, RTT jitter, client loss rate, and server loss rate are in the proportion of 1:2:11:6", i.e., client loss 55%, server loss 30%, RTT jitter 10%, min RTT 5%. Absolute delay "only contribute[s] 1/20 of the influence". — [INFOCOM 2006 PDF](https://people.cs.nycu.edu.tw/~chuang/pubs/pdf/2006infocom.pdf)
- Causality caveat in the same paper: "logically, the relation of cause and effect cannot be clarified from a cross-sectional study, we assume the correlation… implies that premature departures are caused by unfavorable network experience". — [INFOCOM 2006](https://people.cs.nycu.edu.tw/~chuang/pubs/pdf/2006infocom.pdf)
- **Chen, Huang, Lei, IEEE TPDS 2009 (full text).** Same trace, logistic model. Sessions with loss ≤ 1% averaged 160 min vs 70 min for loss > 1%. Within the first 10 minutes, 3% of low-loss players left vs 20% of high-loss players. "The network quality can explain 32% of the variability of a player's decision to leave a game after playing for 10 minutes", via lp = 12.5·rtt.mean + 86.1·rtt.sd + 1.1·log(closs) + 1.2·log(sloss). The revised intolerance ratio is "1:2:4:3 approximately" (latency 10%, jitter 20%, client loss 40%, server loss 30%). "The influence of network quality on players declines over time", plausibly because social bonds take over. Avoiding TCP-induced jitter (18 → 32 ms) would cut premature departure "from 20% to 8%" in simulation. — [TPDS 2009 PDF (NTU repository)](https://scholars.lib.ntu.edu.tw/bitstreams/97b3bee4-0e88-4151-a435-c349f562814a/download)
- **Inconsistency:** the 2006 conference paper says 1:2:11:6, and the 2009 journal and CACM versions say 1:2:4:3. The model formulation differs (Cox hazard vs logistic on 10-min departure), so the weights depend on the model. — [INFOCOM 2006](https://people.cs.nycu.edu.tw/~chuang/pubs/pdf/2006infocom.pdf); [TPDS 2009](https://scholars.lib.ntu.edu.tw/bitstreams/97b3bee4-0e88-4151-a435-c349f562814a/download)

### Inferences
- Variability (jitter) and loss matter more than mean latency for interactive apps. Wi-Fi labels should be built on jitter and loss distributions per test, not only on mean RTT or throughput.
- Behavioural sensitivity to network is strongest at session start (early departure). That is also the only window in which a short scripted phone test can observe behaviour.

### Gaps
- Claypool's real-user retention studies and modern cloud-gaming churn data (Stadia, GeForce Now, Xbox Cloud) were not found or opened in this pass. Most of Claypool's work is lab-based user studies, which are out of scope here.
- The CACM article ("How sensitive are online gamers to network quality?") returned HTTP 403.

## 5. Methodology: causality, confounders, turning behaviour into labels, and caveats for small samples

### Takeaway
The large-n literature offers three tools: (1) quasi-experimental matched pairs that hold content, device or connection and geography fixed (Krishnan); (2) explicit confounder splits (content type, device, connectivity) that change the model itself, not just its intercept (Balachandran); and (3) randomization where possible (Puffer, Google). All of them show that behavioural signals are heavy-tailed and weak per session. They work as labels only when aggregated over very many sessions, or when reduced to a binary "failure or severe impairment" event.

### Cited Findings
- **Matched design.** Krishnan & Sitaraman match each treated view with an untreated view that has "identical values for the confounding variables": content, connection type (mobile, DSL, cable, fiber) and geography. They then use the non-parametric sign test. The authors say this "must not be viewed as a definitive proof of causality" but increases confidence "by eliminating the effect of major confounding factors". — [Krishnan & Sitaraman 2012](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~ramesh/Site/HOME_files/imc208-krishnan.pdf)
- **Trade-off in matching.** "adding too many matching parameters can highly reduce the availability of matches, eventually impacting the statistical significance". For rebuffering they also force the untreated view to have watched "to at least the same point" as when the treated view was first impaired. This avoids the survivorship bias of long sessions accumulating more stalls. — [Krishnan & Sitaraman 2012](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~ramesh/Site/HOME_files/imc208-krishnan.pdf)
- **Confounders change the curve.** Connectivity changes tolerance (wireless users tolerate buffering more), live vs VoD changes the whole model, and splitting by confounders added about 18% accuracy. — [Balachandran 2013](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2013/papers/sigcomm/p339.pdf)
- **Turning behaviour into labels.** Labels in this literature are: engagement as fraction viewed binned into classes (Balachandran), PCR from 1–2 star ratings (VIA, Hosseinkashi), premature departure inside 10 minutes (Chen TPDS), return within one day or week (Krishnan), daily searches per user (Brutlag) and call duration (Chen 2006, Gupchup). Every one is binarized or binned, and every one is evaluated on populations. — sources above
- **Survivorship and early-exit ambiguity.** Short sessions mix "sampling" and "quality-caused quit", and Balachandran dropped them. Long sessions accumulate more impairments, which Krishnan normalised by video duration. — [Balachandran 2013](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2013/papers/sigcomm/p339.pdf); [Krishnan & Sitaraman 2012](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~ramesh/Site/HOME_files/imc208-krishnan.pdf)
- **Power.** Puffer: ±13–21% CI on stall ratio with 2.5 years of video per arm. The engagement gain came only from users watching more than 3 hours. — [Puffer 2020](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/nsdi20-paper-yan.pdf)
- **Survey design shapes the label.** Trigger rate, cool-down and number of survey pages measurably change the 5-star rate. — [Hosseinkashi 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.00652)
- **Per-user models generalize worse.** Attendee-specific rating prediction underperformed, and cross-organization transfer dropped accuracy. — [Hosseinkashi 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.00652)
- **Aggregation hides dynamics.** VIA uses call-averaged RTT, loss and jitter and acknowledges hidden bursts. Hosseinkashi blames aggregated telemetry for the AUC ceiling. Chen finds RTT standard deviation more important than mean RTT. — [VIA](https://cs.cmu.edu/~junchenj/via.pdf); [Hosseinkashi 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.00652); [TPDS 2009](https://scholars.lib.ntu.edu.tw/bitstreams/97b3bee4-0e88-4151-a435-c349f562814a/download)

### Inferences
- For small samples (tens to hundreds of room tests), free behaviour (watch time, abandonment, return) is statistically useless as a label. The effects in the literature are 0.2–5% shifts that needed 10^6–10^7 sessions or randomized multi-year trials. One user per room in a scripted test also has no freedom to "abandon".
- What transfers to small samples is the **set of application-level events and thresholds** these studies found to drive behaviour: startup > 2 s, buffering-event rate > ~0.3/min, BufRatio in 0–10%, negative bitrate switches, failures, RTT > 320 ms, loss > 1.2%, jitter > 12 ms, call reliability events. They can be measured objectively on every test.
- The quasi-experimental logic carries over at small scale as **within-subject matched comparisons**: same phone, same user, same time window, different room or building. This is the analogue of Krishnan's matched pairs and removes device and user confounders, which this project already knows matter (2.4 GHz client confound, per-building efficiency).

### Gaps
- I found no study that validates behavioural labels against per-session subjective ratings on the same sessions at scale. Hosseinkashi comes closest (telemetry → rating AUC 0.74).
- I found no large-n behavioural QoE study specific to **home Wi-Fi** (as opposed to cellular or WAN) with rooms or position as the treatment.

## Implicações para o projeto

Concrete, prioritized actions supported by the findings above. They assume the agreed phone-app MVP plus subjective label (meeting 2026-10-28) and the room-ruler work.

1. **Do not use free engagement behaviour (watch time, abandonment, return, session length) as the label. Use app-side impairment events instead.** Evidence: behavioural effects are 0.2–5% per session and needed 6.7M viewers / 23M views (Krishnan), 40M sessions (Balachandran), 430M calls (VIA), 63k users and 38.6 years of video with ±13–21% CIs (Puffer), or Google-scale randomized traffic (Brutlag). In a scripted test the user cannot abandon. The phone app should log, per test, the **objective events these studies found to drive behaviour**:
   - Video (YouTube or ExoPlayer test): startup/join time, number and duration of stalls, buffering ratio, buffering events per minute, number of **downward** bitrate/resolution switches, time at each resolution, playback failure. Use the thresholds to binarize: startup > 2 s (Krishnan); buffering events > 0.3/min (Balachandran); BufRatio bins of 1% in 0–10% (Dobrian); any downward switch (Plakia, abstract-level evidence).
   - Calls (WebRTC loopback or a real call app): per-second RTT, loss and jitter, **not just call averages**. Apply the VIA "poor network" thresholds (RTT > 320 ms, loss > 1.2%, jitter > 12 ms) as a first label. Log reliability events (setup failure, drop, one-way audio or video), which Gupchup ties most strongly to call duration and Hosseinkashi treats as a no-ML binary flag.
   - Interactive/gaming-like probe: RTT standard deviation and loss bursts. Chen finds jitter about 2× and loss 4–11× more influential than mean RTT.
2. **Make labels binary or ordinal with explicit thresholds, and model with trees or classifiers.** Every large study binarizes (PCR = 1–2 stars; departure < 10 min; 10-bin engagement classes), and Balachandran shows linear regression underperforms decision trees because of thresholds and non-monotonicity. This is consistent with the project result that the direct classifier (0.76) beats the regression ruler (0.65).
3. **Keep the subjective rating but treat it as noisy, and design it the way Skype and Teams learned to.** Collapse 1–5 into poor (1–2) vs not poor. Add an optional problem-token page after low ratings (Gupchup: ~54% of poor-call raters answered). Avoid prompting too often: Hosseinkashi found a high trigger rate without cool-down skews ratings toward 5 stars, and a single-page survey spreads them out. Even at Microsoft scale, telemetry predicts poor ratings only at AUC ≈ 0.74, so expect a ceiling near there, not above 0.9.
4. **Use within-subject matched comparisons (QED-style) to handle confounders.** Pair tests with the same phone, user, app and time window but different room or AP band. Then count pairs where the "worse" room really has worse events, and apply a sign test as Krishnan did. This directly targets the known confounders (client device / 2.4 GHz, per-building efficiency) and needs far fewer samples than population regression.
5. **Record confounders as first-class fields and allow the model to differ by them.** Balachandran's top three confounders map to this project as follows: content type → test type (video vs call vs web); device → phone model and Wi-Fi chipset/band; connectivity → AP model, building and WAN type. Krishnan also shows users on better connections (fiber) are less patient. Home Wi-Fi users on fiber plans may judge harshly, so log the subscriber's plan and WAN type.
6. **Keep per-second time series, not only per-test means.** The AUC ceiling in Teams is attributed to aggregated telemetry. VIA notes hidden loss bursts, and Chen finds RTT variance outweighs mean RTT. This also supports the earlier memory finding that router counters are ~11 s speedtest-window deltas: label and feature windows must align in time.
7. **Sample-size expectations for planning.** For a binary "impairment event occurred" label with a base rate of a few percent (Puffer: 4% of streams stalled; Dobrian: 7% of views with BufRatio > 10%), a few hundred tests yield only a few dozen positives. Plan deliberate stress conditions (far rooms, 2.4 GHz, interference) so the poor class gets enough positives, rather than relying on naturally occurring stalls. Behavioural engagement effects at the sizes reported (≤ 5%) are not detectable at project scale, even across the hotmilk and villa buildings combined.
8. **Lower priority: passive engagement in a long-running app.** If the app later runs in the background on real users' phones for weeks, Krishnan-style return/failure signals and Chen-style early departure become feasible, but only with thousands of users and matched designs. It is not an MVP path.

Caveats on transferring these results: almost all of these studies are WAN or CDN or cellular, not in-home Wi-Fi. Their users chose the content, which carries interest effects, whereas the project's tests are scripted. Several key effect sizes are correlational (Dobrian, VIA, Chen), and only Puffer and Google are randomized.
