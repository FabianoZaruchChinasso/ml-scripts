## 1. How should V2 collect user labels? (question wording, scale, timing, pairwise vs absolute, how many raters and ratings)

### Takeaway
Collect **event-triggered, episode-specific** ratings in the moment, not global opinions of a room. Pair a short absolute question (5-level ACR plus a yes/no acceptability item) with **forced-choice pairwise comparisons between rooms in the same home**. Pairwise answers are less noisy and faster per sample, and they cancel the per-home scale offset that produced "442 Mbps = Ruim". The standards' rater counts per stimulus (24 in a lab, 35 in a public environment, at least 8 per stimulus in crowdsourcing) cannot be reached in a home. So V2 data must be treated as P.913 "pilot-level" data per room, with statistical power coming from **many repeated comparisons by the same residents over weeks** and from **more homes**, not from more raters per room.

### Cited Findings

**Experience sampling (ESM) protocols**
- Consolvo et al. define the three ESM schedules: interval-contingent (fixed times), event-contingent (after events of interest) and signal-contingent (random prompts). They add context-triggered sampling, where sensors fire the prompt. Computerized ESM lets the evaluator "precisely control the amount of time a participant has to respond to a prompt before the prompt is recorded as missed, thus ensuring that recall bias has been minimized". In one of their studies, context-triggered sampling collected 3,458 in-situ questionnaires from 16 participants, and in interviews participants "had difficulty remembering where they had been in the past few days". — [Consolvo et al., "Conducting In Situ Evaluations for and With Ubiquitous Computing Technologies", Int. J. Human-Computer Interaction, 2007](https://makeabilitylab.cs.washington.edu/media/publications/Consolvo_ConductingInSituEvaluationsForAndWithUbiquitousComputingTechnologies_InternationalJournalofHuman-ComputerInteraction2007.pdf) (full text read. The venue and year come from the file and title page. DOI not verified on the PDF.)
- Event-contingent sampling matters "in case target events are rare, short-lasting, or unpredictable, in which case periodic sampling might completely miss them". If participants do not want to be interrupted, they "are likely to introduce the recall bias by delaying their answers until they find a suitable moment to respond", so the schedule has to fit the user's routine. — [Pejovic, Lathia, Mascolo, Musolesi, "Mobile-Based Experience Sampling for Behaviour Research", arXiv 1508.03725, 2015 (book chapter)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.03725) (full text read)
- **The closest QoE precedent: Ickin et al. 2012.** The study ran 4 weeks (Feb-Mar 2011) with about 30 recruited Android users, 2 of whom dropped out. The ESM survey appeared "after using an application ... not after each application usage, but at random times after random application usage, with a maximum of 8-12 surveys per day". It asked for an application QoE rating from poor [1] to excellent [5], citing the ITU MOS scale, plus location, social context and mobility. Each ESM took "approximately 5 s". Users were asked to "provide independent ratings, while keeping in mind that a rating is a purely subjective QoE, episodic assessment". The study collected **7,804 ratings, an average of 9.29 per day per participant**. High ratings (4, 5) were "much more frequent than low ones". A weekly Day Reconstruction Method interview explained the ESM answers and caught inconsistencies. — [Ickin, Wac, Fiedler, Janowski, Hong, Dey, IEEE Communications Magazine 50(4):48-56, 2012, DOI 10.1109/MCOM.2012.6178833](https://access.archive-ouverte.unige.ch/access/metadata/e1c94ce2-e423-4b5a-bcbd-20c700c79199/download) (full text read)
- **A field trial with an in-app QoE feedback tool: Casas et al.** It had 30 participants on their own smartphones for 2 weeks, giving per-session feedback through "a customized QoE crowd-sourcing app" and reporting location (home, metro, etc.). The trial collected "almost 700 ratings". Participants were paid in vouchers, "which proved to be sufficient for achieving correct involvement". — [Casas, Gardlo, Seufert, Wamser, Schatz, "Taming QoE in Cellular Networks: from Subjective Lab Studies to Measurements in the Field", IRTF/RAIM 2015](https://irtf.org/raim-2015-papers/raim-2015-paper7.pdf) (full text read)

**Scale and question wording**
- The P.913 ACR scale is 5 Excellent / 4 Good / 3 Fair / 2 Poor / 1 Bad, with numbers optional. P.913 §7.2.1 says "Translating labels into different languages does not result in a significant change to the mean opinion score", that unlabelled or numbers-only scales are acceptable, and that "alternative wording of the labels may be used when the rating labels do not meet the needs of the experimenter". — [ITU-T P.913 (06/2021)](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en) (full text read)
- P.913 §7.1.1.1: "ACR ratings confound the impact of the impairment with the influence of the content upon the subject". — [ITU-T P.913](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)
- **ACR with hidden reference (P.913 §7.2.2).** The reference version is rated as a "freestanding stimulus ... like any other", and the score is turned into a differential score DV = V(PVS) - V(REF) + 5. — [ITU-T P.913](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)
- **Instructions (P.913 §11.5).** Use the "same exact process, form of words and interactions for each participant". Do not give feedback on performance. Reassure participants "that there is no right or wrong answer". Training is "a crucial part of this method, since subjects could misunderstand their task". — [ITU-T P.913](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)
- **Training in P.808 crowdsourcing (§6.3.1.2).** Training samples should "approximately cover the range from worst to best quality to be expected in the test", without telling participants they are the best and worst. Every worker gets the same training stimuli. Training access "should expire within 60 minutes" and never last more than 24 hours. — [ITU-T P.808 (06/2021)](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.808/en) (full text read)
- **An acceptability item alongside ACR.** Casas et al. asked a continuous ACR question plus a binary "whether they would continue using the application under the corresponding conditions or not". Acceptance separated conditions sharply (23% for non-adaptive YouTube at 1 Mbps vs about 99% with DASH). Maximum MOS values "are never 5 but somewhere between 4.2 and 4.6", which the authors call "rating scale saturation". — [Casas et al. RAIM 2015](https://irtf.org/raim-2015-papers/raim-2015-paper7.pdf)

**Pairwise vs absolute**
- **Mantiuk et al. 2012.** The paper compared single stimulus, double stimulus, forced-choice pairwise and pairwise similarity judgments on images. Groups were 17 observers for the rating methods and 11 for the pairwise methods, each repeating the experiment 3 times on different days. Forced-choice pairwise "results in the smallest measurement variance". It is "the most time-efficient if used in combination with a sorting algorithm that reduces the number of comparisons". Single and double stimulus did not differ. "Methods that require a simpler task from observers tend to give more coherent results". Retrospective power analysis found that ranking needs **26-38 measurements (observers x repetitions) for larger quality differences and 29-66 for smaller ones**. These figures hold for a single t-test and are higher once multiple comparisons are corrected. The authors warn that results "do not necessarily generalise to differently designed experiments". — [Mantiuk, Tomaszewska, Mantiuk, Computer Graphics Forum 31(8):2478-2491, 2012](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rkm38/pdfs/mantiuk12cfms.pdf) (full text read)
- **Shah et al. 2014.** In seven MTurk tasks, collecting ordinal (pairwise) data directly gave lower per-sample error than converting cardinal scores to orderings: 29/6/40/13/17/20/22% vs 31/18/46/17/46/31/27%. Ordinal tasks were also faster, for example 31 s vs 70 s for age and 84 s vs 305 s for distance. Ordinal measurements "avoid calibration issues ... such as the evaluators' inherent (and possibly time-varying) biases, or tendencies to give inflated or conservative evaluations". They "typically provide less information" per answer. With 5 workers per task, the ordinal estimator won on Kendall's tau in all three tasks tested (0.277 vs 0.129, 0.547 vs 0.085, 0.513 vs 0.304), but it lost on squared L2 error in the spelling task, where ordinal and cardinal noise were similar. The suggested guideline is to estimate the noise of both methods "by eliciting a few samples where the ground truth is known" and to prefer ordinal when its noise is "low enough". — [Shah, Balakrishnan, Bradley, Parekh, Ramchandran, Wainwright, "When is it Better to Compare than to Score?", arXiv 1406.6618, 2014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.6618) (full text read; arXiv preprint)
- **Shah et al. 2015/2016.** For Bradley-Terry-Luce and Thurstone models, minimax error depends on the comparison graph through its Laplacian spectrum. Ordinal and cardinal error rates have "identical scalings apart from constant pre-factors". A degree-k expander needs n >= kd samples where a complete graph needs n >= d^2, so "for small sample sizes we should prefer" sparser well-connected designs. — [Shah et al., "Estimation from Pairwise Comparisons: Sharp Minimax Bounds with Topology Dependence", arXiv 1505.01462 (published in JMLR 17, 2016, venue from my prior knowledge and not verified on the arXiv copy)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.01462)
- **Pérez-Ortiz & Mantiuk 2017.**
  - Scaling: 1 JOD means 75% of observers chose one condition, and 2 JOD means 91%.
  - Unanimous answers make naive scaling distances infinite, which is "common in experiments". Their MLE plus a prior "reduces estimation error when the number of observers is low".
  - Design: compare conditions that are close in quality so that they "are confused in certain number of cases". Unequal designs across observers are "generally non-advisable". With no prior ordering, use sorting or active sampling.
  - Ties: "Our general recommendation is to run two-alternative-force-choice experiments without ties".
  - Software: the `pwcmp` Matlab toolbox. The R packages BradleyTerry2 and others support ties and incomplete designs.
  — [Pérez-Ortiz & Mantiuk, "A practical guide and software for analysing pairwise comparison experiments", arXiv 1712.03686, 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.03686) (full text read)
- Pérez-Ortiz et al. (2019/2020) argue for mixing rating (coarse scale) with pairwise comparison (fine discrimination) on one unified scale. — [catalog record](https://katalog.dhi-paris.fr/vufind/Record/NLM300880448/Details) (abstract only, carried over from the earlier notes)

**How many raters and ratings**
- **P.913 §9.1.**
  - "At least 24 subjects must be used for experiments conducted in a controlled environment", meaning every stimulus rated by at least 24 subjects after screening. "At least 35 subjects must be used for experiments conducted in a public environment".
  - Pilot studies "to indicate trending" should use 8-12 participants and "must be clearly labelled as being pilot studies".
  - "One rating from each of 24 subjects should yield approximately the same accuracy as three ratings from each of nine subjects", but this "would not be appropriate when the goal of the experiment is to characterize differences among subjects".
  - Analysing demographics or environment factors needs the subject count "increased dramatically (e.g., by a factor of 10)".
  - A "public environment" is any setting where people not involved in the experiment are present, so a home with family around arguably qualifies.
  — [ITU-T P.913](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)
- **P.808 crowdsourcing.**
  - Each ACR stimulus should be "assessed at least by 8 individuals and each circuit condition with 96 votes". Satisfactory lab-crowd correlation was seen with 10 stimuli per job and 24 workers per stimulus.
  - A rating task should take "a couple of minutes" and hold 5-15 stimuli. Include "one gold standard and one trapping question for each 10 stimuli".
  - Discard a submission if any gold or trap is failed. Flag votes with |z| > 3.29 as potential outliers. Remove all of a participant's responses if they fail the conditions "more than twice".
  — [ITU-T P.808 (06/2021)](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.808/en)

### Inferences
- **The unit of labelling should be the episode, not the room.** ESM practice (Ickin; Consolvo; Pejovic) and the recency finding in the earlier notes all point to rating a specific, just-finished use: "the video you just watched here", "the call you just ended". A room-level label then comes from aggregating many episodes, in the same way the ruler aggregates many speed tests. This also gives each room many ratings per rater, which P.913's 24x1 ≈ 9x3 trade-off allows when the goal is the stimulus (the room) and not characterising raters.
- **A concrete V2 prompt design, drawn from the sources above and not tested as a package:**
  1. **Trigger (event-contingent).** Prompt after a video or call session longer than about 1-2 min, after an app-run YouTube or WebRTC probe, and after a detected disconnect. The disconnect trigger is the only way to sample "Ausente" episodes. Cap prompts at about 8-12 per day as in Ickin (fewer is safer for an operator's customers), and expire a prompt after a few minutes so late answers are logged as missed rather than recalled.
  2. **Q1, ACR in pt-BR, 5 levels.** "Como foi a qualidade do [vídeo/chamada/navegação] que você acabou de usar aqui?", with Excelente / Boa / Razoável / Ruim / Péssima. P.913 §7.2.1 allows translated and alternative wording. Ickin shows about 5 s per answer is achievable.
  3. **Q2, acceptability (binary).** "Do jeito que estava, você continuaria usando normalmente?" (Casas et al.). Acceptability separated conditions better than MOS there, and it maps to the project's decision boundary of usable vs not usable.
  4. **Q3, a concrete-symptom check that doubles as a gold item.** "O vídeo travou?" or "A chamada congelou?". The answer can be checked against the app's own logs for the same session, which plays the role of P.808's gold question without fake stimuli.
  5. **Q4, pairwise, occasional, forced choice without ties (Pérez-Ortiz).** "Comparando com [cômodo X], aqui o [vídeo/chamada] costuma ser melhor ou pior?". Ask it only for rooms the person has actually used recently, and pick rooms adjacent in the current estimated ordering (sorting or adaptive design) instead of a full design.
- **Pairwise comparisons fix the specific failure seen so far.** With one rater per home, every absolute label carries that home's offset and range. A within-home comparison is invariant to both by construction, and Shah et al. show that it is also less noisy per sample and quicker to answer. The cost is the loss of absolute anchoring, so it must be combined with Q1/Q2 or with the objective application labels from the main report.
- **Budget for comparisons (own normal-approximation calculation, α = 0.05 two-sided, power 0.8).** Detecting that one room is preferred 75% of the time (1 JOD) takes about **29 comparisons per room pair**. At 91% (2 JOD) it takes about **9**, and at 64% it takes about **98**. These figures agree with Mantiuk's 26-66 measurements. A home with 5 rooms has 10 pairs. Comparing only the 4 adjacent pairs at a 1 JOD resolution therefore needs about 115 comparisons per home. At 3-5 comparison prompts per day, that is roughly 4-6 weeks per home, or less with several residents. This is a planning estimate, not a published rule.
- **Raters per home.** P.913's 24/35 per stimulus are out of reach, so V2 room-level results should be reported explicitly as "pilot/trending" in the P.913 §9.1 sense. Recruiting every resident (often 2-4) is still worth it for one reason: it is the only way to separate rater offset from building effect (section 2). The earlier notes' rule that "each new building is worth more than more rooms" still holds for between-building questions.
- **Shared anchors across homes.** The range effect comes from each rater seeing only their own home. Following P.808's training rule (cover the expected range, give everyone the same training stimuli), the app could play the same short clips to every rater at enrolment, for example a clean clip, one with a 3 s stall and one with repeated stalls, and have them rated. These common stimuli give a shared reference for estimating each rater's offset (section 2). They calibrate the scale only for video impairments, not for Wi-Fi experience in general.
- **The hidden-reference analogy.** ACR-HR's DV = V(PVS) - V(REF) + 5 is the standards' version of the project's "relative to the best room" ruler. If the near-router position is rated in the same session as a hidden reference, a DMOS-like room score is principled. It inherits the weakness noted in the main report, though: a bad reference distorts every other room's score.

### Gaps
- The van Berkel et al. 2017 CSUR survey and the Hektner et al. ESM book were not accessible, so no ESM-specific numbers on optimal prompts per day, expiry windows or study length are cited beyond Ickin's 8-12/day and about 5 s per answer.
- No source compared pairwise vs ACR for **network or Wi-Fi QoE** in homes. All pairwise evidence comes from images, audio and MTurk estimation tasks.
- No source quantified how often phone users will answer a pairwise "room A vs room B" question from memory. Unlike Mantiuk's side-by-side images, both rooms cannot be experienced at once, so this is a remembered comparison and carries its own recall bias.
- The Consolvo & Walker 2003 primary paper was not read. Its claims are known only through the 2007 paper's summary of ESM.

## 2. Multi-annotator and label-noise methods (Dawid-Skene, Raykar, Frénay & Verleysen): what works with this little data?

### Takeaway
Annotator models (Dawid-Skene, Raykar et al.) estimate per-rater confusion matrices by **cross-referencing several raters on the same items**. With one rater per building and no shared items, the rater's error process cannot be identified at all, and Frénay & Verleysen note that label-noise models are generally unidentifiable without priors. Published validations use roughly 100-20,000 items and 39-762 workers. For the current 27 labels these methods are not usable. For V2 they become usable only if residents overlap on the same rooms or anchors, and then preferably in a Bayesian form with informative priors.

### Cited Findings
- **Raykar et al. 2010.**
  - The method jointly learns a classifier, each annotator's sensitivity and specificity (a confusion matrix for multi-class labels) and the hidden true labels by EM. It covers binary, categorical, **ordinal** (via Frank & Hall's K-1 binary decomposition) and regression labels.
  - Beta priors on sensitivity and specificity give MAP estimates. Majority voting is the initialisation.
  - Majority voting "assumes all experts are equally good".
  - In the experiment, 5 simulated radiologists (2 experts, 3 novices) labelled 2,115 biopsy-proven cases with 27 features. The learnt classifier was "as good as the one learnt using the golden ground truth", with AUC about 3.5% above majority voting.
  - Stated assumptions, which "are not true in practice": annotator performance does not depend on the instance, and errors are independent given the truth. The model "doesn't estimate difficulty of items".
  — [Raykar, Yu, Zhao, Valadez, Florin, Bogoni, Moy, "Learning From Crowds", JMLR 11:1297-1322, 2010](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume11/raykar10a/raykar10a.pdf) (full text read)
- **Dawid-Skene 1979, through secondary descriptions.** The model estimates individual observer error rates for polytomous labels "even when the patient's 'true' response is not available", using EM. — [Dawid & Skene, JRSS C 28(1):20-28, 1979, DOI 10.2307/2346806](https://doi.org/10.2307/2346806) (not accessible; description from the Zendy/RePEc record via search). Raykar et al. call their binary algorithm "similar to the one proposed by Dawid" but say it adds features and priors. — [Raykar et al.](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume11/raykar10a/raykar10a.pdf)
- **Small-data behaviour of Dawid-Skene.** The Dawid-Skene estimator "maximizes a non-convex log-likelihood function" and EM "can get trapped in a local optimum". Zhang et al. propose spectral initialisation followed by EM. The spectral stage requires partitioning "the workers into three disjoint and non-empty groups" and third-order moments. Their real datasets ranged from 108 items / 39 workers / 4,212 labels (Bird) to 19,033 items / 762 workers (TREC). The 5-class Web relevance set had 2,665 items and 177 workers. — [Zhang, Chen, Zhou, Jordan, "Spectral Methods meet EM: A Provably Optimal Algorithm for Crowdsourcing", arXiv 1406.3824, 2014 (NIPS 2014; JMLR 2016, venue from prior knowledge)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.3824) (abstract, method and dataset table read)
- **Frénay & Verleysen survey.**
  - Taxonomy: noisy completely at random (NCAR), noisy at random (NAR, depends on the true class only) and noisy not at random (NNAR, depends on the features too). Mislabelling near the class boundary is NNAR, "the most general case".
  - Label noise is a common result of **subjective** labelling tasks and of inter-expert variability.
  - Identifiability: in a simple survey-error example "it is possible to obtain an infinite number of maximum likelihood solutions for the true proportions of answers and the error probabilities", and "several works claim that prior information is strictly necessary".
  - Prevalence bias: with a 10% minority class and 5% mislabelling, the observed minority share is 14%, an overestimate of 40%.
  - Validation: "since validation data are also polluted by label noise, methods like e.g. cross-validation or bootstrap may poorly estimate generalization errors and choose metaparameters that are not optimal", which the authors call "an important open research question".
  — [Frénay & Verleysen, "Classification in the Presence of Label Noise: a Survey", IEEE TNNLS 25(5):845-869, 2014, DOI 10.1109/TNNLS.2013.2292894](https://romisatriawahono.net/lecture/rm/survey/machine%20learning/Frenay%20-%20Classification%20in%20the%20Presence%20of%20Label%20Noise%20-%202014.pdf) (full text read from a third-party copy that carries the IEEE header. The UCLouvain repository copy is restricted: [record](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/handle/2078.5/254107).)
- **P.913's QoE-specific "soft rejection" (§12.6).** It jointly estimates true quality, subject bias and subject consistency by an iterative bias-removed, consistency-weighted MOS. P.913 presents it as a generalisation of the §12.4 bias removal and recommends it for "challenging test conditions" such as crowdsourcing, with a Python reference in Appendix III. — [ITU-T P.913](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)

### Inferences
- **Today, with 1 rater per building and 0 shared items, nothing can be estimated.** A rater's confusion matrix is learnt from disagreement with other raters on the same items. No item has two raters, so the Dawid-Skene, Raykar and P.913 §12.6 models collapse to "trust the single label". This is Frénay's identifiability problem in its purest form. Any claimed label-noise correction on the 27 labels would be fully driven by the prior.
- **The noise is plausibly NNAR**, depending on the room's own features. The "442 Mbps = Ruim" label depends on the home's range, and the "Ok" class is used for borderline rooms. NNAR noise is the hardest case and is not corrected by symmetric-noise methods.
- **When V2 makes these models usable:**
  1. Two or more residents rating the **same rooms** in each home. The minimal multi-annotator design is overlap within the home.
  2. The shared enrolment anchors from section 1, which link raters **across homes**.
  3. A Bayesian ordinal version with informative priors on rater accuracy, such as Raykar's beta priors or a hierarchical prior over rater offsets.
  Even then, the scales in Zhang et al. (at least 108 items and dozens of workers) suggest these models will only be weakly identified with a few dozen rooms, so they should be used to **report uncertainty**, not to "clean" labels.
- **A simpler and more defensible alternative for V2:** the additive subject model from the earlier notes (P.913 §12.4/§12.6, SUREAL), applied **within each home** across residents and anchors. It needs only overlap, not three worker groups.
- **Do not clean or remove "noisy" labels.** With 27 labels and 3 "Ok", dropping suspect labels removes a large share of a class. Janowski & Pinson (earlier notes) and Frénay both warn that screening can remove legitimate disagreement.

### Gaps
- The Dawid-Skene original was not read. No study was found on its behaviour with fewer than about 50 items or with one rater per item, and the expected answer (non-identifiable) is inferred from model structure, not cited.
- No source was found on annotator models for **ordinal QoE labels with few raters** beyond Raykar's binary decomposition and P.913's Gaussian model.

## 3. How should the project evaluate models against the labels? (ordinal mixed models with few groups, leave-one-group-out, permutation, Bayesian hierarchical)

### Takeaway
With 6 buildings the honest evaluation is **leave-one-building-out with every fold reported, a within-building permutation test for the ordering signal, and ordinal (not linear) models**. A building random effect should be fit with a weakly informative half-t/half-Cauchy prior: Gelman shows that flat priors overestimate group SDs below about 5 groups, and published ordinal-multilevel simulations start at 25 clusters. Cross-validated accuracy on 27 noisy labels carries error bars of roughly ±0.2, so no ruler-vs-ruler difference under about 0.1-0.2 is interpretable.

### Cited Findings
- **Bauer & Sterba 2011.**
  - Fitting a multilevel linear model to ordinal outcomes "is shown to be inferior in virtually all circumstances".
  - PQL "often performs as well as ML when used with ordinal data" and is "typically superior to ML when the data include a small to moderate number of clusters (i.e., <= 50 clusters)". PQL fixed effects and variance components were still negatively biased in many cells.
  - The simulation design used J = 25, 50, 100 or 200 clusters, so nothing below 25 was tested.
  — [Bauer & Sterba, "Fitting Multilevel Models With Ordinal Outcomes", Psychological Methods 16(4):373-390, 2011, DOI 10.1037/a0025813](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/2472/2017/04/Bauer.Sterba.2011.pdf) (abstract and design read)
- **Gelman 2006.**
  - Recommendations: "starting with a noninformative uniform prior density on standard deviation parameters ... will generally work well unless the number of groups J is low (below 5, say). If J is low, the uniform prior density tends to lead to high estimates of σα".
  - When more prior information is wanted, use the half-t family, with "a reasonable starting point" being a half-Cauchy whose scale is "high but not off the scale".
  - Inverse-gamma(ε, ε) is not recommended because inferences are sensitive to ε when σ is near zero.
  - A proper posterior under the uniform prior needs at least 3 groups.
  — [Gelman, "Prior distributions for variance parameters in hierarchical models", Bayesian Analysis 1(3):515-533, 2006](http://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/research/published/taumain.pdf) (full text of recommendations read)
- The R `ordinal` package fits cumulative link mixed models (`clmm`), using the Laplace approximation by default and adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature, which is more accurate for small clusters or a large random-effect variance. — [ordinal manual](https://cran.r-universe.dev/ordinal/doc/manual.html) (documentation, carried over from the earlier notes and the search summary)
- **Varoquaux.**
  - Cross-validation error bars "are often underestimated". Simple experiments show "±10% for 100 samples".
  - "The standard error across folds strongly underestimates them". Leave-one-out and repeated random splits give similar spread.
  - The binomial distribution of errors gives a lower bound on the spread.
  — [Varoquaux, "Cross-validation failure: small sample sizes lead to large error bars", arXiv 1706.07581, 2017 (NeuroImage 180:68-77, 2018, venue from prior knowledge)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.07581) (full text read)
- **Ojala & Garriga 2010.** Test 1 permutes labels to test whether a classifier found "a real class structure". Test 2 permutes features within classes to test whether it exploits feature dependencies. Permutation p-values give a classifier-agnostic significance test of cross-validated performance. — [Ojala & Garriga, "Permutation Tests for Studying Classifier Performance", JMLR 11:1833-1863, 2010](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume11/ojala10a/ojala10a.pdf) (abstract and test definitions read)
- **P.913 on statistical units (§12.4).** "HRCs must not be compared using the distribution of individual ratings ... the number of data points N is artificially inflated, and the statistical test will indicate a level of sensitivity that is not supported by the experimental data". — [ITU-T P.913](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)
- Validation labels carry the same noise as training labels, so cross-validation can misestimate generalisation and mis-tune hyperparameters. — [Frénay & Verleysen](https://romisatriawahono.net/lecture/rm/survey/machine%20learning/Frenay%20-%20Classification%20in%20the%20Presence%20of%20Label%20Noise%20-%202014.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Size of the error bars (own calculation).** The binomial standard error of an accuracy near 0.5 with n = 27 is sqrt(0.25/27) ≈ 0.096, so a 95% interval is about ±0.19. That is before clustering by building, which makes it wider. Varoquaux's ±10% at n = 100 scales to roughly ±20% at n = 27. The 0.045 gap between the fixed and relative rulers in the main report sits deep inside this noise.
- **Statistical unit.** By analogy with P.913's warning about pooling individual ratings to compare HRCs, comparisons between rulers or models should treat **buildings** (or rooms, for room-level metrics) as the unit, never individual speed tests or individual V2 ratings.
- **Recommended evaluation stack for 6 buildings (and more in V2):**
  1. **Leave-one-building-out (LOBO)** for any fitted model, reporting all fold scores. For absolute-label metrics, accept that the held-out rater's offset is unknowable.
  2. **Within-building pairwise concordance** (fraction of correctly ordered room pairs, or per-building Kendall τ) as the primary metric, because it is offset-invariant.
  3. **Restricted permutation (Ojala Test 1, restricted to within-building).** Shuffle labels among rooms of the same building, recompute the concordance, and repeat about 10,000 times. The p-value then answers "does the ruler order rooms within a home better than chance?" without assuming independence between buildings. With few rooms per building, the permutation null is coarse (for example 3! = 6 orderings for a 3-room home), so report the exact null size.
  4. **A descriptive CLMM** `label ~ score + (1|building)`, fit with adaptive quadrature, plus a Bayesian equivalent with a half-t or half-Cauchy prior on the building SD and a prior-sensitivity check (half-normal vs half-Cauchy scale). Report the building SD only as qualitative. Bauer & Sterba give no evidence for J = 6 and Gelman warns about J < 5.
  5. **Never** fit a linear model to the 5-level labels (Bauer & Sterba), and never pick the best of several rulers on the same 27 labels without a pre-registered choice.
- **What V2 changes.** With per-episode ratings, the natural model becomes `rating ~ score + (1|rater) + (1|room) + (1|building)`. Rater and building effects become separable only where a building has 2 or more raters or shared anchors exist. The building count still sets the power for any between-building claim.

### Gaps
- No simulation study of CLMM (or any ordinal multilevel model) with as few as 6 clusters was found. Bauer & Sterba start at 25, and Gelman's guidance is for normal hierarchical models.
- No source gives guidance on permutation tests specifically for within-cluster rank concordance with tiny clusters. The design above is my own adaptation of Ojala & Garriga.
- Bürkner & Vuorre's ordinal regression tutorial (AMPPS 2019) was not downloaded (PsyArXiv returned HTML), so brms-specific advice is not cited.

## 4. Filling gaps: SOS hypothesis, Hoßfeld TMM 2014 crowdtesting, P.913 bias-removal clause, Casas 2015 thresholds

### Takeaway
P.913 §12.4 bias removal is now verified in the primary text. It **requires every subject to rate a common set of stimuli** and keeps the bias when comparing with user descriptions, so it cannot be applied to one-rater-per-building data. The SOS hypothesis is verified only through the formula restated by its authors' group. The Casas thresholds are verified in primary texts (YouTube about 4 Mbps, Facebook 1 Mbps, Gmaps and web browsing about 2 Mbps, WhatsApp 2 Mbps), along with a correction to the participant demographics in the earlier notes. The TMM 2014 crowdtesting paper remains inaccessible.

### Cited Findings
**P.913 §12.4 and §12.6 (primary text read)**
- Subject bias is "the difference between the average of one subject's ratings (one subject, all PVSs) and the average of all subjects' ratings". It is estimated as μΔi = (1/Ji) Σj (oij - μψj), where μψj is the MOS of PVS j across subjects, and removed as rij = oij - μΔi. This "does not impact MOS" but "reduces the standard deviation of ratings". — [ITU-T P.913](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)
- When bias may be removed: "when the analysis focuses on MOS comparisons, then bias should be removed". "When the analysis compares objective or subjective data with user descriptions (e.g., from blogs, forums or questionnaires), then MOS and subject bias should be taken into consideration (i.e., it cannot be removed)". "When the analysis focuses on subject behaviour, then the analysis can focus only on subject bias". — [ITU-T P.913](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)
- §12.6 calls the advanced method a "bias-removed consistency-weighted MOS", or "soft rejection", as opposed to the "hard rejection" of §11.4 post-screening, and says it generalises §12.4. — [ITU-T P.913](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)
- This confirms the earlier notes' secondary clause numbers (§12.4 bias removal; §12.6 covering bias plus inconsistency, as the Amazon ZREC paper said). The 06/2021 edition was the latest listed on the ITU page when accessed. Its download ID ended in "-W", so its in-force status should be rechecked on the ITU page.

**SOS hypothesis**
- As restated by the same group, on a scale [U-, U+] the maximum possible SOS for a MOS u is S+(u) = sqrt(-u^2 + (U- + U+)u - U-U+). The SOS hypothesis posits SOS(u)^2 = a · S+(u)^2. The PDF extraction renders this as "S(u) = a · S+(u)" with the square root garbled, so the exact placement of the square root should be checked. The parameter a ∈ [0, 1] is "scale invariant when linearly transforming the user ratings", but different scales shown to participants may give different a. a "depends on the application or service, and the test conditions, and is derived from subjective tests". — [Hoßfeld, Heegaard, Varela, Möller, "Formal Definition of QoE Metrics", arXiv 1607.00321, 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.00321) (full text read)
- The original paper: Hoßfeld, Schatz, Egger, "SOS: The MOS is not enough!", QoMEX 2011, pp. 131-136, DOI 10.1109/QoMEX.2011.6065690. It "postulates a square relationship between the MOS and the SOS" and claims validation "for a wide range of studies". — [IEEE Xplore 6065690](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6065690) (abstract only. The per-application a-values were not found in any accessible source.)

**Hoßfeld et al. TMM 2014 crowdtesting**
- Bibliographic details: Hoßfeld, Keimel, Hirth, Gardlo, Habigt, Diepold, Tran-Gia, IEEE TMM 16(2):541-558, 2014, DOI 10.1109/TMM.2013.2291663. Per search summaries, it covers rater reliability, incentives and payment, unknown environment and a two-stage design. — [catalog record](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c354t-167991087317e99b6a8ce9da18f8a10c734cfb21e60883629f38120e5044337f3) (**full text not accessible**: the [mediaTUM PDF](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/doc/1189575/1189575.pdf) returned an Anubis bot-protection page)

**Casas et al. thresholds (primary texts read)**
- **Lab (RAIM 2015).** There were 52 participants (29 female, 23 male, average age 32, 40 under 30, about half students). Ratings used a continuous ACR scale plus acceptability, through a questionnaire that popped up "immediately after a condition has been tested". — [Casas et al. RAIM 2015](https://irtf.org/raim-2015-papers/raim-2015-paper7.pdf). **Correction:** the earlier notes said "mean age about 23" from a search summary. The RAIM paper says average age 32.
- **YouTube.** Tested at 1/2/4 Mbps. With DASH, QoE was near-optimal across all tested conditions. The non-adaptive player stalled heavily at 1 Mbps, where acceptance was 23% vs about 99% with DASH. 4 Mbps is "high enough to reach near optimal results". — [RAIM 2015](https://irtf.org/raim-2015-papers/raim-2015-paper7.pdf); [Casas, Seufert, Wamser, Gardlo, Sackl, Schatz, "Next to You: Monitoring Quality of Experience in Cellular Networks From the End-Devices", IEEE TNSM 13(2):181-196, 2016, DOI 10.1109/TNSM.2016.2537645](https://doi.org/10.1109/TNSM.2016.2537645) (full text read from a repository copy)
- **Gmaps.** Tested at 1-16 Mbps. 4 Mbps gave MOS ≈ 4.5 "and from this value on, QoE saturation already occurs". 2 Mbps gave good quality and almost full acceptance, and 1 Mbps "rapidly brings Gmaps into bad user experience". **Facebook.** 0.5 Mbps gave fair quality with about 80% acceptance, 1 Mbps good quality with almost full acceptance, and 8 Mbps excellent. — [RAIM 2015](https://irtf.org/raim-2015-papers/raim-2015-paper7.pdf)
- **Web browsing (Chrome, news site).** Tested at 0.5/1/2/16 Mbps. MOS ≈ 4 at 2 Mbps and only ≈ 4.15 at 16 Mbps, while the slowest condition still gave MOS ≈ 3.5 and about 90% acceptance. **WhatsApp (5 MB file).** Good above 2 Mbps, "very bad quality" at 500 kbps, saturation after 4 Mbps. The authors note these results are "partially biased by both the specific file size used in the tests and the participants task briefing". — [Casas et al. TNSM 2016](https://doi.org/10.1109/TNSM.2016.2537645)
- **Field.** 30 participants, 2 weeks, about 700 ratings. For YouTube, "MOS > 4 for almost all sessions with MFT > 5 Mbps", but between 1 and 4 Mbps the picture is "very fuzzy", with MOS from 2 to 5, because YouTube QoE "is strictly linked to the stallings". The authors conclude that "it's necessary to additionally perform measurements at the application layer". They "could not find any relevant correlation between the strength of the signal and the MOS scores". — [RAIM 2015](https://irtf.org/raim-2015-papers/raim-2015-paper7.pdf)

### Inferences
- **P.913 §12.4 cannot be applied to the current labels, for two reasons.** (a) μψj needs several subjects per PVS (room), and each room has one rater. (b) The project's goal includes "will this person complain", which is the "compare with user descriptions" case where P.913 says bias **cannot** be removed. Per-building centering (earlier notes) is therefore a project-specific analysis choice, not a standards-sanctioned bias removal. It should be labelled as such.
- **What the SOS hypothesis implies for the labels.** Even a perfectly measured room has a spread of ratings set by a, and the spread is largest mid-scale (Fair/"Ok") and shrinks toward the ends. This matches the project's tiny, unstable "Ok" class. Mid-scale labels are the least reproducible, which is a further reason to collapse to fewer classes or to use acceptability.
- **Casas' thresholds sit 2-3 orders of magnitude below the project's typical throughputs.** Their own field data show that throughput explains QoE only at the extremes and that signal strength showed no correlation. For home Wi-Fi rooms running at hundreds of Mbps, these thresholds act only as **floors** (consistent with the main report). The discriminating signal has to come from application-layer events (stalls, freezes) and availability.

### Gaps
- The SOS a-values per application (web, video and so on) and the full SOS paper were not accessible.
- The TMM 2014 two-stage design and its unreliable-worker percentages remain unverified.
- The SIGCOMM All Things Cellular 2015 paper itself was not read. Its content is taken from the TNSM 2016 journal version, which covers the same five apps.

## Implicações para o projeto (prioritized actions)

### Takeaway
Stop trying to extract more from the 27 single-rater labels. Rebuild V2 labelling around in-the-moment episode ratings plus within-home pairwise comparisons with overlapping raters, and evaluate with within-building, permutation-based and ordinal methods that admit how few clusters there are.

### Cited Findings
The actions below rest on the findings above. Each cites its main source.

- **P0.1, relabel the current analysis honestly.** Call the per-building centering a project choice, not P.913 bias removal ([P.913 §12.4](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)). Treat every current result as pilot/trending in the P.913 §9.1 sense. Report accuracy intervals of about ±0.19 for n = 27 (own binomial calculation; [Varoquaux](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.07581)).
- **P0.2, use the evaluation stack from section 3.** LOBO with per-fold scores. Within-building pair concordance as the primary metric. A within-building restricted permutation test ([Ojala & Garriga](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume11/ojala10a/ojala10a.pdf)). An ordinal (never linear) model ([Bauer & Sterba](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/2472/2017/04/Bauer.Sterba.2011.pdf)) with a half-t or half-Cauchy prior on the building SD ([Gelman 2006](http://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/research/published/taumain.pdf)).
- **P0.3, do not run Dawid-Skene, Raykar or label cleaning on the current labels.** They are not identifiable with one rater per item ([Frénay & Verleysen](https://romisatriawahono.net/lecture/rm/survey/machine%20learning/Frenay%20-%20Classification%20in%20the%20Presence%20of%20Label%20Noise%20-%202014.pdf); [Raykar et al.](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume11/raykar10a/raykar10a.pdf)).
- **P1.1, V2 ESM triggers.** Prompts are event-contingent: after video or call sessions, after app-run probes and on detected disconnects. Cap them at about 8-12 per day or fewer, expire them after a few minutes, and aim for about 5 s per answer ([Ickin et al. 2012](https://access.archive-ouverte.unige.ch/access/metadata/e1c94ce2-e423-4b5a-bcbd-20c700c79199/download); [Consolvo et al. 2007](https://makeabilitylab.cs.washington.edu/media/publications/Consolvo_ConductingInSituEvaluationsForAndWithUbiquitousComputingTechnologies_InternationalJournalofHuman-ComputerInteraction2007.pdf); [Pejovic et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.03725)). Log the trigger, timestamp, device, band/BSSID and the app's own session metrics with every answer.
- **P1.2, V2 question set.**
  - Q1: episode ACR in pt-BR, 5 levels ([P.913 §7.1.1, §7.2.1](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)).
  - Q2: binary acceptability ("continuaria usando assim?") ([Casas et al.](https://irtf.org/raim-2015-papers/raim-2015-paper7.pdf)).
  - Q3: a symptom question checkable against app logs, which serves as the gold item ([P.808 §6.3.1, §6.4.1](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.808/en)).
  - Q4: an occasional forced-choice pairwise question between recently used rooms, with no ties ([Pérez-Ortiz & Mantiuk](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.03686); [Shah et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.6618)).
  - Show identical written instructions to everyone, with no performance feedback ([P.913 §11.5](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)).
- **P1.3, shared enrolment anchors.** Every rater in every home rates the same 3-5 short reference clips (clean, one stall, repeated stalls) at enrolment and perhaps monthly. This gives the cross-home common stimuli that bias estimation needs ([P.808 training rule](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.808/en); [P.913 §12.4](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en); range-effect anchors in the [Qualinet white paper](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/107476/107476.pdf)).
- **P1.4, rater overlap.** Recruit every willing resident so that at least 2 people rate the same rooms in each home. This is the minimum design under which rater offset and building effect separate, and under which P.913 §12.6 or SUREAL-style models apply ([P.913 §12.6](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.913/en)).
- **P1.5, pairwise budget.** Plan about 29 forced-choice comparisons per adjacent room pair to resolve a 75%-preference difference, or about 9 for 91%. That is roughly 115 per 5-room home, or about 4-6 weeks at 3-5 prompts per day (own calculation, consistent with [Mantiuk 2012](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rkm38/pdfs/mantiuk12cfms.pdf)'s 26-66). Use an adjacent or sorting design, not the full design ([Pérez-Ortiz & Mantiuk](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.03686); [Shah et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.01462)). Scale the comparisons with Thurstone/BTL MLE plus a prior (`pwcmp` or BradleyTerry2).
- **P1.6, pre-register the label definition.** Decide before collection how episode ratings aggregate to a room label (for example the median of Q1 plus the share of Q2 "no"), the class collapse (usable / degraded / unusable, with Ausente separate), and the primary metric. Mid-scale ratings are the least reproducible ([SOS formulation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.00321)), and selecting among rulers after seeing the labels inflates results.
- **P2.1, after V2 data exist.** Fit `rating ~ score + (1|rater) + (1|room) + (1|building)` as a Bayesian CLMM, and use multi-annotator models only to report label uncertainty ([Raykar et al.](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume11/raykar10a/raykar10a.pdf); [Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.3824)). Validate router-feature models against application-layer objective labels first, since Casas found throughput alone "very fuzzy" in the mid range ([Casas et al.](https://irtf.org/raim-2015-papers/raim-2015-paper7.pdf)). Use human ratings for within-home ordering validation.
- **P2.2, recruiting.** Add buildings before adding rooms. Every between-building claim is bounded by cluster count, and ordinal multilevel estimators are untested below 25 clusters ([Bauer & Sterba](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/2472/2017/04/Bauer.Sterba.2011.pdf)).

### Inferences
- **Most important change.** Moving from "one person labels each room once" to "several residents rate many episodes, with occasional within-home comparisons and shared anchors" turns the label problem from unidentifiable into merely small-sample. Every method in sections 2 and 3 needs that structure.
- **What the 27 labels can still do.** They remain useful as a qualitative sanity check (does any ruler get the clearly bad rooms?), but not to choose between rulers.

### Gaps
- No published in-home Wi-Fi study combining ESM ratings, pairwise room comparisons and router telemetry was found. The protocol above is assembled from adjacent fields (mobile-app ESM, image-quality pairwise comparison, speech/video crowdsourcing) and needs a pilot in 1-2 homes before roll-out.
- Response rates and fatigue for operator customers (as opposed to paid study participants, as in Ickin and Casas) are unknown and could be much lower.
