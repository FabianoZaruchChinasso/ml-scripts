# Measurement bias from failed and missing tests in home Wi-Fi QoE data

Project context for the report writer: speed tests that fail (disconnection, timeout) are never written to the dataset. A kitchen that users call "unusable" shows 43 successful tests and 0 failures, and its labels come from the medians of those successful tests. The dataset therefore holds only the tests that completed, and the label inherits that selection.

## 1. What the speed-test methodology critiques say about Wi-Fi as a confounder and about test failures

### Takeaway
All four classic critiques agree that home Wi-Fi and the client device are often the real bottleneck, and that user-initiated tests suffer from self-selection. None of them treats failed or aborted tests as data. They either discard them silently (Bauer et al. drop "broken" tests; M-Lab flags short or small tests; SamKnows aborts tests when cross-traffic is present) or never discuss them. The "survivors only" pattern in this project is the community's default, not a project-specific mistake, and the literature offers almost no ready-made correction for it.

### Cited Findings
**Feamster & Livingood, "Measuring Internet Speed: Current Challenges and Future Recommendations"**, Communications of the ACM 63(12):72–80, Dec 2020. Preprint: arXiv:1905.02334 ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02334v3); [CACM](https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/2020/12/248801-measuring-internet-speed)).
- "Speed tests that are run over a home wireless connection often reflect a measurement of the user's home wireless connection, not that of the access ISP, because the WiFi network itself is usually the lowest capacity" link. They list distance to the AP and signal strength, device/AP limitations, other users of the spectrum, and non-Wi-Fi interference as factors. — [Feamster & Livingood](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02334v3)
- They cite Sundaresan et al.: "whenever downstream throughput exceeded 25 Mbps, the user's home wireless network was almost always the bottleneck." They add that the study dates from 2013 but "the general trend of home wireless bottlenecks is still prevalent." — [Feamster & Livingood](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02334v3)
- Router-embedded versus device-over-Wi-Fi Ookla tests run back to back on the same network: "the throughput reported from the user's mobile device on the home network is almost half of the throughput that is reported when the speed test is taken directly from the router." — [Feamster & Livingood](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02334v3)
- On self-selection bias: "many users initiate such tests only when they are experiencing a technical problem or are reconfiguring their network"; for example, a user tests over Wi-Fi, moves the AP, and tests again. — [Feamster & Livingood](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02334v3)
- On infrequent testing, they use the analogy of looking out the window once a day in the evening and concluding it is always dark. Their remedy: "Automatic tests run multiple times per day at randomly selected times during peak and off-peak times can account for some of these factors." — [Feamster & Livingood](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02334v3)
- Recommendations include: retire NDT for speed testing (single TCP connection, slow start included); use native, embedded or dedicated measurement; "control for factors along the end-to-end path when analyzing results"; augment active tests with application quality metrics; adopt standard, open methods. — [Feamster & Livingood](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02334v3)
- The paper does **not** discuss failed, aborted or timed-out tests as a source of bias. Its notion of bias is about *when* and *why* users test, not about which attempts survive into the data. — [Feamster & Livingood](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02334v3) (full text checked)

**Sundaresan, de Donato, Feamster, Teixeira, Crawford, Pescapè, "Broadband Internet Performance: A View From the Gateway"**, ACM SIGCOMM 2011, pp. 134–145, DOI [10.1145/2018436.2018452](https://doi.org/10.1145/2018436.2018452) ([PDF](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p134.pdf)).
- Tools run from end hosts "cannot analyze the effects of confounding factors such as home network cross-traffic, the wireless network, or end-host configuration". One-time tests also "cannot establish a baseline performance level or observe how performance varies over time." — [Sundaresan et al. 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p134.pdf)
- Their table of confounders and mitigations lists "Wireless Effects → Use a wired connection to modem" and "Cross Traffic → Measure cross traffic and avoid it/account for it." — [Sundaresan et al. 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p134.pdf)
- SamKnows (the FCC Measuring Broadband America platform) checks cross-traffic before each test: if it "exceeds 64 Kbits/s down or 32 Kbits/s up … it aborts the test." This is a deliberate conditioning rule: tests exist only for quiet moments. — [Sundaresan et al. 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p134.pdf)
- Scale: about 4,000 gateways across 8 ISPs (SamKnows, 4,200 devices from Dec 2010 to Jan 2011) plus BISMark gateways that run both passive and active measurements. — [Sundaresan et al. 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p134.pdf)

**Sundaresan, Feamster, Teixeira, "Home Network or Access Link? Locating Last-Mile Downstream Throughput Bottlenecks"**, PAM 2016, DOI [10.1007/978-3-319-30505-9_9](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-30505-9_9). Only the abstract and summary were seen.

**Bauer, Clark, Lehr, "Understanding Broadband Speed Measurements"**, TPRC 2010 (MIT working paper) ([PDF via M-Lab](https://www.measurementlab.net/publications/understanding-broadband-speed-measurements.pdf)).

- In the lab, the tools mostly agree except on high-latency paths, where Ookla reports higher throughput. ### Inferences
- The project's "store successes only" policy matches what M-Lab, Bauer et al. and the FCC MBA program do. The difference is that those programs study ISP access links, where a failure is mostly noise. In a per-room Wi-Fi study, failure *is* the phenomenon. Copying their filtering habit transfers a reasonable choice for ISP benchmarking into a context where it removes the signal.
- The SamKnows abort-on-cross-traffic rule is a useful analogue. Conditioning a test on "conditions are good enough to start" produces a sample that represents good moments. A test that only completes when the link survives roughly 10 s of saturating transfer is the same kind of conditioning.
- Two Wi-Fi metadata fields that the IMC 2022 paper shows are decisive (band and RSSI) are exactly what a failed test usually loses if nothing is written on failure.
- Self-selection works in both directions here. Users test more when something is wrong (Feamster, Bauer), but in a room that is "unusable" a test may not even start or finish. Diagnostic over-testing inflates the bad tail, while survivorship erases it, so the net bias cannot be signed without the attempt log.

### Gaps
- I found no speed-test methodology paper that reports the *rate* of failed or aborted tests, or that analyzes how dropping them shifts medians.
- The 2023 MBA Technical Appendix ([data.fcc.gov](https://data.fcc.gov/download/measuring-broadband-america/2023/Technical-Appendix-fixed-2023.pdf)) was not read, so it is unknown whether current MBA still computes test availability or failure rates.
- Ookla's public methodology on failed or partial tests was not checked.

## 2. Survivorship / MNAR bias and treating failures as censored or as their own outcome

### Takeaway
Network measurement has mature tools for one piece of this, namely estimating outage or dropout *rates* with exposure-time denominators and survival-style hazard estimates (Padmanabhan et al. 2019). It also has tools for regional sampling-bias reweighting of crowdsourced tests (Lee et al. 2023). I found **no** network-measurement paper that models failed throughput tests as censored observations (Tobit or survival on throughput) or as an explicit outcome class. That part has to be borrowed from general statistics and justified locally. For the kitchen case, the defensible move is to model "attempt failed" as its own outcome with a proper denominator, rather than to impute a throughput value.

### Cited Findings
**Padmanabhan, Schulman, Levin, Spring, "Residential Links Under the Weather"**, ACM SIGCOMM 2019, pp. 145–158 ([PDF](https://thunderping.cs.umd.edu/papers/sigcomm19-weather.pdf); [CAIDA page](https://www-old.caida.org/publications/papers/2019/residential_links_under_weather/)).
- A dropout is when an address "responsive to pings from at least three vantage points transitions to being unresponsive from all of the vantage points for 11 minutes". The three-vantage-point requirement avoids "classifying in-network faults as residential link faults." — [Padmanabhan et al. 2019](https://thunderping.cs.umd.edu/papers/sigcomm19-weather.pdf)
- The denominator is the *responsive address hour*, meaning an hour in which the host was up at the start and "therefore had the opportunity to dropout". The hazard is estimated as λ̂ = D / Σ O_i, where D is dropout-hours and O_i the observed responsive hours per address, explicitly borrowed from survival analysis. They note that "hosts that remain responsive can naturally be observed for longer periods of time than those that drop out." — [Padmanabhan et al. 2019](https://thunderping.cs.umd.edu/papers/sigcomm19-weather.pdf)
- Multiple dropouts in one hour count as one "dropout-hour". The goal is "to avoid biasing our results towards hosts that repeatedly fail and recover over a short period of time." — [Padmanabhan et al. 2019](https://thunderping.cs.umd.edu/papers/sigcomm19-weather.pdf)
- Their reporting rule for a bin: n ≥ 40 and nλ̂(1−λ̂) ≥ 10. For example, estimating one dropout-hour per month (λ̂ = 0.00139) needs 7,205 samples. Below that, bins are not reported. — [Padmanabhan et al. 2019](https://thunderping.cs.umd.edu/papers/sigcomm19-weather.pdf)
- To separate weather effects from background failures, they subtract a baseline dropout probability (an additive difference in hazards). They admit this gives correlation, not attribution of cause. — [Padmanabhan et al. 2019](https://thunderping.cs.umd.edu/papers/sigcomm19-weather.pdf)
- "Not all dropouts are necessarily outages": a momentary re-attach with a new address (PPP re-association, renumbering) also looks like a dropout. Dropouts are a superset of failures. — [Padmanabhan et al. 2019](https://thunderping.cs.umd.edu/papers/sigcomm19-weather.pdf)
- The dataset spans eight years, 101 billion responsive pings and 10.8M addresses. Wireless link types (WISP, satellite) show "orders of magnitude greater" weather-driven increases in failure than wired links. — [Padmanabhan et al. 2019](https://thunderping.cs.umd.edu/papers/sigcomm19-weather.pdf)

**Lee, Paul, Gupta, Belding, Gu, "Analyzing Disparity and Temporal Progression of Internet Quality through Crowdsourced Measurements with Bias-Correction"**, arXiv:2310.16136 (2023, stat.AP; abstract only).
- They analyzed about 1M Ookla tests linked to Census block groups and found significant regional sampling bias with a homogeneity test. They propose two population-based correction methods. The bias had a small effect on city-level distributions, and it "may slightly overstate" the speed increase over time. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16136)
- A search snippet attributes a "propensity of testing … Hajek-type inverse probability weighting" correction to this research line. I did not verify that phrase in the paper itself. — [search result pointing to arXiv 2310.16136 / related](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.16136) (unverified)

**Paul, Liu, Adarsh, Gu, Gupta, Belding, "Characterizing Performance Inequity Across U.S. Ookla Speedtest Users"**, arXiv:2110.12038 (2021; abstract only).
- Median download speeds differ by more than 150 Mbps between states, and urban/rural gaps are significant in all but one state. The abstract does not discuss sampling bias or failures. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.12038)

**General statistics (NOT opened in this session; named only so the writer can point to the standard references):**
- Rubin (1976, *Biometrika*), "Inference and missing data", is the origin of the MCAR/MAR/MNAR taxonomy. Little & Rubin, *Statistical Analysis with Missing Data*, is the textbook. Tobin (1958, *Econometrica*) introduced censored regression (Tobit). Heckman (1979, *Econometrica*) introduced the selection-correction two-step. — not verified in this session; cite with care.
- From a source that *was* returned, at overview level only: right-censoring in reliability testing records both failure times and "time-of-test-termination for those that did not fail", and highly censored data (more than 50%) make many tests perform poorly. — [Wikipedia: Censoring (statistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censoring_(statistics)) (secondary)

### Inferences
- **Missingness mechanism for the kitchen case.** A test fails *because* link quality is bad (disconnection, timeout). That is missing-not-at-random by construction: whether the outcome is observed depends on its own value. Conditioning on success yields a truncated sample (worse than censored, because the failures are not even counted). The 43/0 record tells you the success rate is *unknown*, not 100%. Without the number of attempts, no reweighting or censoring model can be fitted, since all of them need the denominator.
- **Why Tobit or left-censoring is a weak fit here.** Treating a failure as "throughput < c" (left-censored at a detection floor) assumes the failure is just a very slow test on the same throughput scale. Wi-Fi failures are often a different process: association or roaming loss, DHCP, the app being killed, or a server timeout. A failure can also happen at decent PHY rates during a roaming event. Padmanabhan's caveat that "dropouts are supersets of failures" is the network-measurement version of this warning. A censored-throughput model is only defensible for failure codes that clearly mean "transfer started but too slow", such as a timeout with partial bytes and no disconnect.
- **Better fit: failure as its own outcome class, with a hazard or rate.** Model each attempt as a multinomial or ordinal outcome {failed, completed-poor, completed-ok}, or as two parts: P(success | room, radio state) × throughput | success. This is the "hurdle" or two-part structure. It keeps the success-only medians interpretable as *conditional* on success and adds a failure rate per room. Padmanabhan's exposure-based hazard (events ÷ time at risk) is the direct template for disconnection rates per connected hour.
- **Label rule for the project.** A room label based on "median of successful tests" should be replaced or joined by a rule such as: room is "unusable" if P(fail) > x **or** median-of-successes < y. A room with many failures and good successes must not be labeled good.
- **Small-sample caution.** By Padmanabhan's rule of thumb (nλ̂(1−λ̂) ≥ 10), a failure rate around 10% needs about 110 attempts per room-bin for a stable estimate. With roughly 40 tests per room, failure rates should be reported with wide intervals (e.g., Wilson or Clopper-Pearson) or pooled hierarchically across rooms and buildings.

### Gaps
- No network-measurement paper was found that applies Tobit, censored regression or survival models to *throughput* when tests fail, or that treats failed speed tests as an explicit outcome class. The absence is a finding, but it may also reflect search depth (15–20 queries).
- The bias-correction methods in Lee et al. 2023 were not verified in the full text.
- The original missing-data references (Rubin, Little & Rubin, Tobin, Heckman) were not opened in this session.

## 3. Active versus passive measurement for capturing disconnections, association failures and roaming

### Takeaway
On-demand active tests see only the moments when someone tests and the test survives. Passive or continuous gateway and device telemetry sees the failures themselves: association and connection-setup failures, dropouts, re-attaches. Large passive studies show connection failures are common, not rare (33% setup failure under RSSI-based AP choice in Pei et al.). Gateway placement was the standard answer to home-network confounding (BISMark, SamKnows, HoA). For this project, the collector on the router or phone should log association and disconnect events continuously, independently of the speed test.

### Cited Findings
- **Pei et al., "Why It Takes So Long to Connect to a WiFi Access Point"**, arXiv:1701.02528 (2017), conference paper; venue not named on arXiv (abstract only). Data: about 5M users, 7M APs and 0.4B Wi-Fi sessions from a "WiFi Manager" app (Android and iOS). A machine-learning AP selection cut connection setup failures "from 33% to 3.6%" versus pure signal-strength selection and cut setup time by about 80%. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.02528)
  - A search snippet also claims "45% of connection attempts fail". I did not see that in the abstract; treat it as unverified. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02528)
- **Grover, Park, Sundaresan, Burnett, Kim, Feamster, "Peeking Behind the NAT: An Empirical Study of Home Networks"**, ACM IMC 2013, pp. 377–390, DOI [10.1145/2504730.2504736](https://doi.org/10.1145/2504730.2504736) (abstract-level). BISmark routers in more than 100 homes in 21 countries for over a year observe all traffic between the access link and the home. Findings include crowded parts of the wireless spectrum and "connectivity interruptions" that were more frequent in developing countries. These are interruptions seen passively from the gateway, not from user-initiated tests. — [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/peeking-behind-the-nat-an-empirical-study-of-home-networks/); [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145%2F2504730.2504736)
- **Sundaresan et al. 2011**: BISMark "performs passive and active measurements" at the gateway. They argue that continual measurement from the same gateway is what establishes a baseline. — [Sundaresan et al. 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p134.pdf)
- **Sundaresan et al., PAM 2016 (HoA)**: passive, low-overhead bottleneck localization (home Wi-Fi versus access link) running on gateways. — [NANOG64 slides](https://www.nanog.org/sites/default/files/meetings/NANOG64/1012/20150601_Sundaresan_Home_Network_Or_v1.pdf)
- **Feamster & Livingood 2020**: recommend "native, embedded, and dedicated measurement techniques and devices" (router, Eero, Raspberry Pi, SamKnows whitebox, RIPE Atlas) and automatic tests at random times over user-initiated ones. — [Feamster & Livingood](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02334v3)
- **Padmanabhan et al. 2019**: continuous outside-in active probing (pings from several vantage points every few minutes) can measure outage hazard without any user action. — [Padmanabhan et al. 2019](https://thunderping.cs.umd.edu/papers/sigcomm19-weather.pdf)
- **Baltrunas, Elmokashfi, Kvalbein, "Measuring the Reliability of Mobile Broadband Networks"**, ACM IMC 2014, pp. 45–58 (full text NOT accessible; secondary summary only). According to a CAIDA AIMS 2014 talk summary, they defined connection reliability failure as loss of the IP address requiring context re-establishment, and found mean time between failures varied widely by operator, with frequently failing operators recovering faster. — [CAIDA AIMS-6 report](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/ana/DDC-AIMS-6Report.pdf); [Simula page](https://www.simula.no/research/measuring-reliability-mobile-broadband-networks) (secondary)

### Inferences
- In this project, a test-initiated measurement is structurally blind to "the phone lost the AP for 20 s and the test died." A passive event stream from the AP (associate, disassociate, deauth reason, roam, DHCP) or from the phone (Wi-Fi state changes, supplicant events) captures exactly the events the current dataset drops. This is the same logic by which BISMark and SamKnows moved measurement to the gateway.
- The project memory notes that the router `router_*` counters are about 11 s speedtest-window deltas. That means the router data is *also* gated by test success today. A collector that samples association state and RSSI on a fixed cadence regardless of tests is needed to break the gate. (This is an inference from the project's own notes, not from the literature.)
- Continuous background probing at low rate (pings to the gateway every few seconds, à la ThunderPing but inside the LAN) gives an outage fraction per room-hour cheaply. On-demand throughput tests stay useful for capacity, but only conditional on being connected.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that used Android Wi-Fi framework logs (e.g., disconnect reason codes) or AP syslog events specifically as QoE labels in homes. The Pei et al. data comes from an app, but the per-stage breakdown (scan, auth, assoc, DHCP) was not visible in the abstract.
- I did not verify which TR-181 / TR-069 parameters expose association or disconnect events or counters per station. That needs a check against the TR-181 data model (in scope of another note).

## 4. Recommended logging practices and availability / time-in-good-state QoE metrics

### Takeaway
The literature's working pattern is: keep every attempt in raw data with flags, filter only in downstream views, and report availability (the share of time or attempts in which the service could be used) separately from performance. The FCC tracked how often tests could not run, but kept it out of the performance analysis. M-Lab keeps short or small tests and flags them. ThunderPing reports outage hazard per exposure hour. For a Wi-Fi QoE label, availability belongs *inside* the label, not beside it.

### Cited Findings
- M-Lab pattern: raw rows are kept, a `filter` struct (IsShort, IsSmall, IsError, IsEarlyExit, …) is attached, and `IsValidBest` is computed as a view. Researchers are told to copy the extended subquery and "edit it to fill their needs". Filtering is a documented, versioned view decision (IsValid2021, IsValid2023, IsValidBest), not a collection-time drop. — [M-Lab unified_downloads.sql](https://github.com/m-lab/etl-schema/blob/main/views/ndt/unified_downloads.sql); [extended_ndt7_downloads.sql](https://github.com/m-lab/etl-schema/blob/main/views/ndt_intermediate/extended_ndt7_downloads.sql)
- FCC MBA 2012: "Results of all tests are in the raw bulk data files". Availability, meaning how often tests could not be run, served as a quality metric but "was not used in analysis of broadband performance." — [FCC MBA 2012 Technical Appendix](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20120928140522/http://transition.fcc.gov/cgb/measuringbroadbandreport/2012/Technical-Appendix.pdf) (excerpt-level)
- ThunderPing: outage likelihood = dropout-hours ÷ responsive hours, with confidence intervals and a minimum-sample rule. — [Padmanabhan et al. 2019](https://thunderping.cs.umd.edu/papers/sigcomm19-weather.pdf)
- Feamster & Livingood: "A well documented, public methodology for tests is critical to understanding measurement results" (quoting the FCC). They also recommend complementing speed with application quality metrics such as rebuffering and resolution changes. — [Feamster & Livingood](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02334v3)
- ITU-T E.800 (09/2008) is the in-force recommendation for "Definitions of terms related to quality of service". The official summary page does not show the availability definition, so the exact wording was not verified. — [ITU-T E.800 summary](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-t/rec/e/T-REC-E.800-200809-I!!SUM-HTM-E.htm); [ITU-T E.800 listing](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-E.800)

### Inferences
Concrete logging spec for this project, derived from the sources above:
1. **One row per attempt, written at start.** Fields: attempt id, timestamp, room or position, device, and the reason the test was started (scheduled or user-initiated). Update the row on finish. If the app dies, the row survives with status `started`. This is M-Lab's "keep raw, flag later" applied at the source.
2. **Outcome code (enum):** `ok`, `timeout_no_bytes`, `timeout_partial`, `disconnect_mid_test` (association lost), `no_association_at_start`, `dhcp_or_dns_fail`, `server_error`, `app_killed`/`unknown`. Record bytes transferred and elapsed time even on failure, as M-Lab's IsSmall and IsShort require.
3. **Radio context snapshot at start and at failure:** band, channel, RSSI, PHY rate, BSSID. This is the metadata that Paul et al. show is decisive and that failures currently lose.
4. **Independent passive stream** (not gated by tests): association and disassociation or roam events, plus a low-rate ping to the gateway. From it, derive **availability per room** = share of time (or share of probe intervals) connected *and* with gateway RTT/loss under a threshold. This is the "time in good state" metric.
5. **Scheduled tests at random times**, as Feamster & Livingood recommend, kept separate from user-triggered ones so self-selection can be measured.
6. **Label construction:** room label = f(attempt success rate, availability fraction, conditional throughput or latency medians). Do not use the median of successes alone. Keep separate fields for "could connect", "test completed" and "throughput given completion", so later models can be two-part or hurdle models.
7. **Retroactive check on the existing data:** if attempt start times are anywhere in app logs or router logs (DHCP leases, association logs), reconstruct attempt counts per room to estimate the missing denominator for the kitchen. If they are not, the current labels must be documented as "conditional on success" and the kitchen case flagged as unlabelable.

### Gaps
- The exact E.800 (2008) definitions of availability, accessibility and retainability were not read. The ITU full text or ETSI derivative standards are needed for a formal citation.
- No published standard was found for a Wi-Fi-specific "time in good state" QoE metric (e.g., share of minutes with RSSI above a threshold and no disconnects). Industry metrics probably exist (Wi-Fi Alliance, Broadband Forum TR-398 / TR-181 diagnostics), but they were not searched here.
- Recent connectivity-outage studies beyond ThunderPing (e.g., Richter et al., IMC 2018, on edge outage detection) were not opened.
