## 1. Rater bias and scale usage: how ITU guidance, MOS critiques and subject models treat per-rater offset and inconsistency

### Takeaway
The subjective-quality literature treats each rating as true quality + a stable per-rater offset (bias) + per-rater random noise (inconsistency) + per-stimulus ambiguity. Even trained lab subjects on a 5-point ACR scale repeat their own exact score only about 50-82% of the time, and the noise spans about half the scale. Your 5-level, one-rater-per-building labels therefore carry a large irreducible error, and with one rater per building the rater offset cannot be separated from the building.

### Cited Findings
**Janowski & Pinson, "The Accuracy of Subjects in a Quality Experiment: A Theoretical Subject Model", IEEE Trans. Multimedia 17(12):2210-2224, Dec 2015, DOI 10.1109/TMM.2015.2484963** — [NTIA PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf); [NTIA record](https://www.its.ntia.gov/publications/details?pub=2814)
- Model: the score of subject i on stimulus (PVS) j = true value + subject bias + an error term. The paper splits the error into subject inaccuracy and stimulus (PVS) scoring difficulty, extending the QoMEX 2014 bias+error model. The authors say explicitly that the model describes "the process of a subject giving an answer", not stimulus quality. — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf); [QoMEX 2014 precursor](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/Pinson_QoMEX2014.pdf)
- Data: 17 ACR datasets on the discrete 5-point scale from "Excellent" to "Bad", including a repeated-rating experiment (AGH/NTIA, in a sound-isolation chamber, with 3 and 6 repeats). — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)
- Repeatability: "none of the subjects exactly repeated their prior scores". The probability of repeating one's own most probable score ranged from 50% to 82% across subjects. Measured against the first score, it ranged from 36% to 74%. Even the most repeatable subjects had 18% of scores differ from their own modal score, and the two most repeatable subjects had maximum deviations of 2 points, "half of the scale". — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)
- Per-stimulus spread: ratings for a stimulus "typically span three of the five levels", never all fall on one level, and sometimes span the whole scale. Repeatability was highest (93%) for very bad quality and worse for good quality, so there is "greater agreement ... for bad quality than for good quality". — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)
- Bias: each subject's observed bias is stable (small CIs) and roughly normal with mean 0 and SD 0.34 scale points. 85% of bias values fall in about ±0.5, one quarter of the scale. — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)
- The authors prefer an additive model (stimulus difficulty and subject inaccuracy are independent, so a difficult stimulus is noisy even for a perfect subject) over a multiplicative one. — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)
- Within-subject Spearman correlations across sessions are "only slightly higher than the between-subject correlations". — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)
- Practical conclusions: (a) scoring is "a random process ... not a flaw or fault that can be eliminated". (b) Remove subject bias when comparing MOS between stimuli, because sensitivity usually improves. Keep the bias when comparing against user descriptions (forums, questionnaires). (c) One rating from each of 18 subjects gives about the same accuracy as three ratings from each of 7 subjects. By extension, 24 subjects × 1 rating ≈ 9 subjects × 3 ratings. Pinson et al. recommend 24 subjects for ACR. (d) Repeated sequences are not a valid way to screen subjects. BT.500-style screening tends to reject inaccurate subjects and can widen the CI because n shrinks. — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)
- The paper notes that the ACR scale is ordinal, so the mode is formally the right "true quality" estimate rather than the mean. — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)

**Li & Bampis, "Recover Subjective Quality Scores from Noisy Measurements", arXiv:1611.01715 (v3 Jul 2017; abridged at DCC 2017)** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01715v2); [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.01715)
- Model: raw score = true quality x_e + a subject term ~ N(b_s, v_s²), where b_s is bias and v_s is inconsistency, + a content term ~ N(0, a_c²), where a_c is content ambiguity. Parameters are jointly estimated by MLE with Newton-Raphson-style updates. The problem is nonconvex, so the method finds a local optimum initialized from MOS. CIs come from observed Fisher information. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.01715)
- Motivation: "more picky viewers tend to be biased toward lower scores". Claimed benefits: tighter CIs than MOS, outlier handling "without the need for z-scoring or subject rejection", and graceful handling of missing data. On the NFLX dataset (4 subjects with scrambled scores), BT.500 rejection "was only able to identify part of the corrupted subjects". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01715v2); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.01715)
- Stated limits: the method gives no advantage when individual scores are randomly corrupted, there is no convergence theory, and it covers single-stimulus designs only (pairwise comparison is future work). — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.01715)

**Li, Bampis, Janowski, Katsavounidis, Krasula, "A Simple Model for Subject Behavior in Subjective Experiments", arXiv:2004.02067 (HVEI/Electronic Imaging 2020; v3 May 2021)** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.02067); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.02067)
- Model: U_ijr = ψ_j + Δ_i + υ_i·X with X ~ N(0,1). Δ_i is the subject bias, υ_i ≥ 0 the inconsistency, and mean bias is constrained to 0. Random scoring shows up as a large υ_i, which down-weights that subject. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.02067)
- Relation to ITU-T P.913 (secondary description): P.913 §12.4 bias removal subtracts each subject's mean residual, and P.913 §11.4 lists rejection options without mandating one. The paper's alternating-projection solver "generalizes the ITU-T P.913 post-test screening procedure" by weighting subjects by consistency. Code is in Netflix SUREAL (github.com/Netflix/sureal). — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.02067); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.02067v1)
- Limitation: the model is Gaussian/continuous, and the paper concedes that discrete category ratings raise specific problems it leaves to future work. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.02067)
- An independent Amazon paper (ZREC) says P.913 clause 12.6 is the one standardized procedure that covers both bias and inconsistency. — [ZREC PDF](https://cdn.amazon.science/45/84/cfa6709242b8a2132181609eb2d2/zrec-robust-recovery-of-mean-and-percentile-opinion-scores.pdf) (search summary only)

**SOS hypothesis and "beyond MOS"**
- "SOS: The MOS is not enough!" (QoMEX 2011, Mechelen, 7-9 Sep 2011) argues for reporting the standard deviation of opinion scores (SOS) with the MOS. It posits a square (parabolic) MOS-SOS relationship with a single parameter a, usable for checking dataset reliability and comparing studies. — [IEEE Xplore 6065690](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6065690) (abstract via search snippet; authors not verified on the record)
- Hossfeld, Heegaard, Varela, Möller, "QoE beyond the MOS: an in-depth look at QoE via better metrics and their relation to MOS", Quality and User Experience 1(1), 2016, DOI 10.1007/s41233-016-0002-1. The paper argues that the MOS hides inter-personal differences and that providers need the distribution (share of satisfied users). It proposes metrics for user diversity, rating uncertainty and the ratio of dissatisfied users, and uses the SOS hypothesis to derive quantiles from MOS. — [VTT CRIS](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/qoe-beyond-the-mos-an-in-depth-look-at-qoe-via-better-metrics-and/)
- The companion report (arXiv:1607.00321) defines α-acceptability A_θ = P(U > θ), %GoB (good or better) and %PoW (poor or worse), and gives Matlab scripts. The same group argues that averaging ordinal ratings assumes equal spacing, so MOS differences inherit the problems of category differences. — [ar5iv 1607.00321](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1607.00321) (search summary)

**Scale and context ("range") effect**
- With ACR scoring, "participants tend to use the whole scale for scoring, independent on the absolute quality of the stimuli". If campaign A covers a subset of campaign B's quality range, the best and worst items in each still get similar scores, so A sits on a "stretched" scale. This is the "context"/"range" effect, usually countered by realigning scores with anchor stimuli shared across campaigns. — [Qualinet crowdsourcing white paper, Hossfeld et al. 2014, HAL hal-01078761](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/107476/107476.pdf)
- The white paper also says that participants whose ratings sit systematically above or below the average may "simply" be using the scale differently and are not unreliable. BT.500 outlier screening targets non-systematic deviation. — [Qualinet white paper](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/107476/107476.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Apparent ceiling.** If a trained lab subject reproduces their own modal score only 50-82% of the time on a 5-point ACR scale, then even a perfect predictor of a rater's "true" category cannot expect much more than that exact-match agreement with a single noisy rating. Home Wi-Fi ratings are less controlled, cover only 27 rooms, and come from untrained raters. Against that, ~0.5 balanced accuracy on 5 (or 4 effective) classes is not obviously a failure of the metric. It may be near what one rating per room can support. This bound comes from video-lab data, so it is an analogy, not a measurement for this setting.
- **Confounding.** In the Janowski/Li model, the building effect (real per-building Wi-Fi efficiency) and the rater bias Δ_i are perfectly confounded when each building has exactly one rater. No estimation method can separate them from these data. The "442 Mbps = bad" case is exactly the range effect: that rater stretched the scale over the range their home offers.
- **Two analysis modes, following Janowski & Pinson's guidance.** (a) For "which rooms are worse", remove each building's offset by centering per building, or analyse ranks within the building. (b) For "will this person complain", keep the offset, because the rater's own scale is the target.
- **Bias magnitude.** Per-subject bias SD in lab data is about 0.34 points. If the project's between-building offsets are much larger than this (for example, a full category or more), that points to a mix of real building effects, very different expectations, and the range effect, which lab bias alone would not explain.

### Gaps
- The full texts of ITU-T P.800, P.910, P.913 and P.1401 were not read. Clause numbers come from secondary sources. I found no evidence of a P.913 revision that formally adopts the Li et al. MLE model; the ITU site would need checking.
- The SOS paper's fitted a-values per application, and its exact author list, were not verified against the full paper.
- No source found models per-rater bias for in-home Wi-Fi or network QoE specifically. All subject-model evidence is from video/image/speech lab tests.

## 2. Crowdsourcing and field (in-the-wild) QoE vs lab: how to collect labels well

### Takeaway
Crowd and field QoE can match lab results only with training phases, reliability checks, short tasks and many raters per condition. Field studies collect thousands to tens of thousands of in-situ ratings from tens to hundreds of users, and still report that prediction "remains difficult". The project's 27 single-rater labels are several orders of magnitude below that scale.

### Cited Findings
**Qualinet Task Force white paper, "Best Practices and Recommendations for Crowdsourced QoE - Lessons learned from the Qualinet Task Force 'Crowdsourcing'" (Hossfeld, Hirth, Redi, Mazza, Korshunov, Naderi, Seufert, Gardlo, Egger, Keimel; COST IC1003, 2014; HAL hal-01078761)** — [PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/107476/107476.pdf)
- A crowdsourced video test run without training "strongly disagree[d]" with lab results. With training and quality-assurance checks, agreement was "similar to the agreement between the results from the two laboratories". — [PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/107476/107476.pdf)
- Recommended reliability checks: consistency questions, content questions about the stimulus, gold-standard questions on obvious impairments (for example "did you notice stalling?" on a clip with no stalling), attention tests, and repeated conditions. Rejection should rest mainly on information not tied to the rating itself. — [PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/107476/107476.pdf)
- Post-test checks: apply BT.500 outlier screening to the ratings and also to per-stimulus task time. In one study, a time-variance rule (SD of observation time > 20 s) excluded about 13% of participants. — [PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/107476/107476.pdf)
- Tests should be kept short, and splitting stimuli across campaigns causes range effects that call for shared anchors. — [PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/107476/107476.pdf)
- The companion journal paper is Hossfeld, Keimel et al., "Best Practices for QoE Crowdtesting: QoE Assessment With Crowdsourcing", IEEE TMM 16(2):541-558, Feb 2014, DOI 10.1109/TMM.2013.2291663. Per its abstract, it covers rating reliability, incentives, payment and unknown environment, and recommends a two-stage design that "leads to more reliable results". — [catalog record](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c354t-167991087317e99b6a8ce9da18f8a10c734cfb21e60883629f38120e5044337f3) (full text not accessible)

**ITU-T P.808 (crowdsourced speech ACR)**
- Naderi & Cutler, "An Open Source Implementation of ITU-T Recommendation P.808 with Validation" (Interspeech 2020, arXiv:2005.08138). It is an MTurk toolkit with ACR/DCR/CCR, environment tests, gold and trap questions, rating-pattern checks and a headset test. Crowd MOS was validated against a P.800 lab test and checked for reproducibility. — [ISCA archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2020/naderi20_interspeech.html); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.08138v1); [code](https://www.github.com/microsoft/P.808) (abstract-level)

**Field and in-situ studies**
- Casas, Seufert, Wamser, Sackl, Schatz et al., "Exploring QoE in Cellular Networks: How Much Bandwidth do you Need for Popular Smartphone Apps?", ACM SIGCOMM All Things Cellular 2015. Lab tests covered YouTube, Facebook, Chrome, Google Maps and WhatsApp under varied downlink bandwidth and latency. About 4 Mbps (YouTube) and 1 Mbps (Facebook) gave near-optimal quality. The RAIM 2015 companion reports that the lab mappings "track the QoE provided by users in real networks". Participants were mostly students and staff, mean age about 23. — [RAIM 2015 paper](https://irtf.org/raim-2015-papers/raim-2015-paper7.pdf); [RAIM slides](https://www.irtf.org/raim-2015-slides/fman/casas.pdf); [dblp](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/sigcomm/CasasSWSI15.html) (search summaries only; thresholds not verified in the PDF)
- Boz, Finley, Oulasvirta, Kilkki, Manner, "Mobile QoE prediction in the field", Pervasive and Mobile Computing 59:101039, 2019, DOI 10.1016/j.pmcj.2019.101039. The study had 292 users and 64,036 in-the-wild ratings in Finland, about 74% on Wi-Fi or LTE, framed as a normal-vs-bad classification. It reports a 20% G-mean improvement over baselines, finds that network and non-network features (for example device memory) both matter, and concludes that mobile QoE prediction "remains difficult in field conditions". — [Aalto record](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/mobile-qoe-prediction-in-the-field/)
- A 2020 Quality and User Experience article (DOI 10.1007/s41233-020-00039-w) reports a 4-week living-lab study: 38 Android users, 6,086 experience-sampling ratings, a smartphone logger, and better prediction models when ratings were given within about 15 minutes of app use. It also summarizes Schatz et al. 2011 as assessing mobile broadband quality in-situ and in-lab on laptops. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s41233-020-00039-w) (search snippet only; full text blocked; authors not verified)

### Inferences
- **Collection protocol.** The minimum transferable practices for a home survey are:
  1. A short training/anchoring step shown to every rater, with the same verbal descriptions of each level tied to concrete experiences (video stalls, a call drops, a page takes >N s).
  2. A gold/attention item.
  3. Asking about a concrete recent experience close in time, following the ~15-minute recency finding, instead of a global opinion of the room.
  4. Logging when and in what activity each rating was given.
- **Scale of evidence.** Field studies with 38-292 users and thousands of ratings still find QoE prediction hard. With 27 labels from 6 people, the realistic goal is to sanity-check a ruler, not to train or validate a fine-grained model.
- **Better labels.** Multiple residents per home, or repeated ratings by the same resident at different times, are the cheapest ways to estimate the noise. Per Janowski & Pinson, 3 ratings × fewer people can substitute partly for more people.

### Gaps
- The full text of the TMM 2014 crowdtesting paper (the two-stage design and the percentage of unreliable workers) was not accessible.
- Votes per condition in P.808 were not verified.
- I found no published in-home Wi-Fi per-room subjective-rating study to benchmark against.
- The Schatz et al. 2011 in-situ vs lab laptop study was seen only as a secondary citation.

## 3. Ordinal and relative labels: pairwise and within-home ranking vs absolute ratings, acceptability vs 5-point

### Takeaway
Forced-choice pairwise comparison gives lower measurement variance than single- or double-stimulus rating in image-quality experiments. Distribution-based or acceptability-style metrics (P(U>θ), %GoB, %PoW) are recommended over the MOS for ordinal data. With one rater per home, within-home relative judgments sidestep the rater's offset and range effect, which absolute 5-level labels cannot do.

### Cited Findings
- Mantiuk, Tomaszewska, Mantiuk, "Comparison of Four Subjective Methods for Image Quality Assessment", Computer Graphics Forum 31(8):2478-2491, 2012. Forced-choice pairwise comparison "results in the smallest measurement variance and thus produces the most accurate results". Single- and double-stimulus rating did not differ significantly. The paper also reviews CIs, testing and power analysis. — [Eurographics DL](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/v31i8pp2478-2491); [PDF](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rkm38/pdfs/mantiuk12cfms.pdf) (abstract via search summary)
- Perez-Ortiz, Mikhailiuk, Zerman, Hulusic, Valenzise, Mantiuk (2019/2020), "From pairwise comparisons and rating to a unified quality scale". Each protocol has advantages, and mixing them, with rating for coarse scale and pairwise for fine discrimination, helps. — [catalog record](https://katalog.dhi-paris.fr/vufind/Record/NLM300880448/Details) (abstract-level)
- For paired-comparison designs, the Qualinet white paper recommends screening by checking inversions on pairs that involve the same stimuli. — [Qualinet white paper](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/107476/107476.pdf)
- Janowski & Pinson: the ACR scale is ordinal, so the mode is the formally correct central value. Error terms are large enough that discrete 5-level and continuous scales give similar MOS accuracy. — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)
- The Hossfeld group describes binary acceptance scales as having been used in QoE studies and as "conceptually similar" to 5-point ACR, possibly not giving significantly different results (citing another study I did not open). They define α-acceptability P(U>θ), and their 2016 paper explores the link between quality and acceptance. — [ar5iv 1607.00321](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1607.00321); [VTT CRIS](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/qoe-beyond-the-mos-an-in-depth-look-at-qoe-via-better-metrics-and/) (search summaries)
- Janowski et al. ("Fix your Netflix", NTIA) report that "good or better" MOS is a common minimum recommendation for paid streaming, and that users' tolerance thresholds varied, with one subgroup's threshold between "good" and "excellent". — [NTIA PDF](https://www.its.ntia.gov/publications/download/Janowski-et-al-FixYourNetflix.pdf) (search summary only)
- Agreement is higher at the bad end of the scale (93% repeatability for very bad stimuli) than at the good end. — [Janowski & Pinson PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Evaluate rankings within each building.** Score the metric-based ruler against each rater's within-building ranking of rooms using per-building Kendall τ or Spearman, or the fraction of correctly ordered room pairs, then average over buildings. This removes rater offset and range effect by construction. With 27 rooms, there are roughly 50-60 within-building room pairs in total (the exact number depends on rooms per building), which is more usable evidence than 27 absolute labels.
- **Collapse to a decision boundary where agreement is best.** Ratings agree most at the bad end, and acceptability is easier for laypeople. A binary "usable / not usable", or a 3-level "good / degraded / unusable", will likely show higher and more stable agreement than 5 levels. Two caveats apply: the boundary must be fixed before looking at results, and balanced accuracy on fewer classes is not comparable to the 5-class figure.
- **Treat "Ausente" separately.** It is a coverage failure, not a degree of quality, and should be analysed as its own binary task (detect no service) rather than as the bottom step of an ordinal scale.
- **Collect pairwise judgments if new labels are gathered.** Asking "which room is better for video/calls, A or B?" within a home matches the lowest-variance protocol in the image-quality literature (Mantiuk 2012) and cannot be distorted by a home's internal range.

### Gaps
- I found no direct empirical comparison of pairwise vs ACR vs acceptability reliability for network/Wi-Fi QoE with very few raters. The Mantiuk evidence is from image quality with many observers.
- No quantified mapping from binary acceptability to ACR thresholds was found.

## 4. Learning from noisy labels and small data: noise models, ordinal/mixed models, grouped validation, CIs with few groups, sample sizes

### Takeaway
Sample-size guidance in the subjective-quality literature is about 24 raters per stimulus for ACR (or about 9 raters × 3 repeats). The project has 1 rater per stimulus and 6 groups. The suitable tools are ordinal models with a per-building random intercept (CLMM) and leave-one-building-out validation reported per fold, with uncertainty taken over groups and not rooms. Standard cluster-robust inference is known to over-reject with 5-30 clusters, so with 6 buildings any CI is fragile and should be shown as fold-level spread or an exact or permutation-style statement.

### Cited Findings
**Sample sizes and raters per stimulus**
- Pinson et al. recommend 24 subjects for an ACR experiment. Janowski & Pinson show that 18 subjects × 1 rating ≈ 7 subjects × 3 ratings, and by extension 24 × 1 ≈ 9 × 3. Fewer subjects with repeats averages out subject bias less, so this is unsuitable when the bias itself is of interest. — [Janowski & Pinson PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)
- Comparisons between five sets of 25-34 subjects put the probability of MOS-order inversions below 0.03%, per Pinson et al. as cited by Janowski & Pinson. — [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/PinsonIEEE_TransMM_Dec2015.pdf)
- Sizes of the scales involved: NFLX crowdsourcing had 154 contents and 1,859 stimuli at 20 votes per stimulus in wave 1 and about 290 in wave 2. Li et al. 2020 fit 22 public datasets. — [Li et al. 2020, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.02067)
- Field QoE: 292 users / 64,036 ratings ([Boz et al. 2019](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/mobile-qoe-prediction-in-the-field/)). Living lab: 38 users / 6,086 ratings ([QUX 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s41233-020-00039-w), snippet only).

**Noise models for labels**
- The Li & Bampis / Li et al. subject model (bias + inconsistency + content ambiguity, MLE, SUREAL code) is the standard QoE-specific noise model. It needs several raters per stimulus to identify the parameters, and it is Gaussian, not ordinal. — [Li & Bampis](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.01715); [Li et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.02067)
- P.1401 (01/2020, in force) evaluates objective models against subjective scores while accounting for subjective uncertainty. It covers the epsilon-insensitive RMSE (RMSE*, §7.7), which ignores errors inside the subjective 95% CI; an outlier ratio (errors beyond the 95% CI); and Fisher-z significance tests between correlations (§7.6), with CI computation in Appendix III. — [ITU P.1401 TOC](https://itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-t/rec/p/T-REC-P.1401-202001-I!!TOC-HTM-E.htm); [ITU P.1401 page](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.1401/en); description of RMSE* use in [arXiv 1801.05889](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.05889) (search summary; standard text not read)

**Ordinal mixed models**
- The R `ordinal` package (Christensen) fits cumulative link mixed models (ordered logit/probit) with lme4-like syntax, for example `clmm(rating ~ x + (1|judge))`, which gives a random intercept per judge. Crossed and nested random effects are supported, and estimation uses Laplace or adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature. — [ordinal manual](https://cran.r-universe.dev/ordinal/doc/manual.html); [clmm2 tutorial](https://mirror.linux.duke.edu/pub/cran/web/packages/ordinal/vignettes/clmm2_tutorial.pdf)

**Inference with few groups**
- Cameron, Gelbach, Miller (Review of Economics and Statistics, 2008; NBER t0344): standard cluster-robust tests "can over-reject ... with few (5-30) clusters". They propose a wild cluster bootstrap-t. — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0344.pdf)
- Follow-up work (MacKinnon & Webb; Canay, Santos, Shaikh) shows that even the wild cluster bootstrap can severely under- or over-reject when clusters are very few or unbalanced, and that its theory assumes many clusters. — [QED WP 1404](https://www.econ.queensu.ca/sites/econ.queensu.ca/files/qed_wp_1404.pdf); [QED WP 1315](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/274640/files/qed_wp_1315.pdf) (search summaries)

### Inferences
- **Mixed model.** A CLMM of the form `label ~ ruler_score + (1|building)` is the natural model. The building random intercept absorbs rater offset plus real building effect, which cannot be separated with one rater, and the slope estimates whether the ruler moves labels within buildings. With 6 groups the random-effect variance is poorly estimated. Report it as "large or small", not as a precise number, and consider a Bayesian version with a weakly informative prior on the building SD.
- **Leave-one-building-out (LOBO) is the honest validation.** Rooms within a building share a rater, a router and a scale, so random K-fold over rooms leaks the rater offset. Report all 6 fold scores, not just the mean, since with 6 folds the mean ± SD is close to anecdotal. Note also that predicting absolute labels for an unseen building requires knowing that rater's offset, which LOBO by construction cannot provide. That is a strong argument for within-building ranking metrics (Section 3).
- **CIs with 6 groups.** Do not use room-level bootstrap or room-level Wilson intervals. They treat 27 rooms as independent and understate uncertainty. Use a building-level bootstrap or permutation, for example shuffling ruler scores within each building to test whether the within-building ordering beats chance, and state plainly that 6 clusters is below the 5-30 range where even corrected methods are unreliable.
- **Noise-aware scoring.** Following P.1401's epsilon-insensitive idea, count a prediction one level away from the label as a "soft hit" in addition to exact balanced accuracy. Janowski & Pinson show raters themselves move by one category routinely. Report exact and ±1 agreement side by side.
- **Do not fit flexible models.** With 27 labels, anything beyond a 1-3 parameter monotone mapping (ordinal logit on one ruler score, or thresholds) will overfit the raters' idiosyncrasies. Label-noise-robust deep methods are irrelevant at this scale.

### Gaps
- I did not read primary ML literature on label-noise models (for example Frénay & Verleysen's survey, or Dawid-Skene for multiple annotators). Dawid-Skene-type annotator models need several annotators per item, which the project lacks, so they would apply only after collecting multi-rater data.
- No QoE-specific guidance was found on validation with as few as 6 groups. The econometric few-cluster literature is the closest evidence and is not tailored to classification metrics.
- The Cameron & Miller rule of thumb of about 40 clusters appeared only in a blog and was not verified, so it is not used above.
